NCLEX-RN Master Study Guide (2026 Test Plan)

How to use this guide

This guide merges the topics from your past study guides (cardiovascular, ANS and CNS pharmacology, respiratory, OB/newborn, pediatrics, psych, GI/hepatobiliary, Med-Surg III final topics, critical care) into the eight Client Needs categories of the 2026 NCLEX-RN test plan. Lines marked Watch out are errors you have made or content that was corrected in past drill sessions. Image links open reference diagrams in a new tab.

The original .docx files were not retrievable, so content here was rebuilt from the topics and corrections recorded across those sessions. Verify anything that conflicts with your professor's slides; slides win for course exams.

Exam mechanics: 85–150 items in 5 hours, including 3 six-item case studies and 15 unscored pretest items. The exam stops when the computer is 95% sure you are above or below the passing standard. Length does not predict pass or fail.

Clinical judgment (NCJMM): the six steps every case study tests

flowchart LR
  A[Recognize cues<br/>what matters?] --> B[Analyze cues<br/>what does it mean?]
  B --> C[Prioritize hypotheses<br/>what is most urgent?]
  C --> D[Generate solutions<br/>what should happen?]
  D --> E[Take action<br/>do the top priority]
  E --> F[Evaluate outcomes<br/>did it work?]
  F -.-> A

Read each case-study item for which step it tests. A "Which findings require follow-up?" item is Recognize Cues; "Which condition is the client most likely experiencing?" is Analyze/Prioritize; "Which findings indicate improvement?" is Evaluate.

Priority rules, in the order you apply them

Rule What it means on a question Example
ABCs Airway before breathing before circulation Stridor beats hypotension
Maslow Physiological and safety needs before psychosocial Hypoglycemia before anxiety about discharge
Acute over chronic New change beats a known baseline New confusion beats chronic COPD SpO₂ of 89%
Unstable over stable Unexpected finding beats expected finding Agitation 8 hr post subdural evacuation (test plan sample key)
Actual over potential A problem happening now beats a risk Active bleeding before fall risk
Assess before intervene Unless the scenario already gave you the data Check the site before calling the provider
Least restrictive first Talk, redirect, then chemical/physical restraint De-escalate before restraints

Watch out: Oxygenation comes before notifying the provider. In past drills you were corrected on this for esophageal variceal hemorrhage. Position and apply O₂ first, then call.

Analogy: triage is a burning-house problem. You grab the person not breathing before the person with a broken arm, and you never stop to phone the landlord while someone is choking.

1. Management of Care (15–21%)

The largest single category. Most items here are delegation, prioritization of multiple clients, consent, and legal/ethical scope questions (LaCharity/Hurst material from NUR206).

Delegation and assignment: who can do what

Role Can do Cannot do
RN Assess, teach, evaluate, plan, unstable clients, IV push, blood, initial teaching, discharge teaching — (cannot delegate assessment, teaching, evaluation, or nursing judgment)
LPN/LVN Stable clients with predictable outcomes, reinforce teaching, collect data, meds (PO, IM, subQ), dressing changes, trach suction, catheter insertion, NG feeds Initial assessment, care plans, IV push (most states), blood administration, unstable clients
UAP/AP ADLs, vital signs on stable clients, I&O, ambulation, turning, bathing, feeding (no swallow risk), specimen collection, bed-making Anything requiring assessment, teaching, evaluation, meds, sterile procedures, first post-op ambulation of a high-risk client

Memory aid: the RN owns anything with EAT in it: Evaluate, Assess, Teach. Stable + routine + predictable = can delegate.

Five rights of delegation: right task, right circumstance, right person, right direction/communication, right supervision/evaluation.

Test plan sample: when assigning UAP to ambulate clients, review safety precautions first for the client with Ménière disease (sudden vertigo = fall risk), not the client with presbycusis, sinusitis, or one cataract.

Prioritizing multiple clients

  1. Airway/breathing threat or sudden neuro change (stridor, new agitation after head surgery, new confusion)

  2. Signs of shock or bleeding (hypotension, tachycardia, restlessness)

  3. Unexpected finding for the diagnosis (chest pain in a post-op client)

  4. Expected finding for the diagnosis (LLQ pain with diverticulitis, internal rotation after knee replacement is less urgent than neuro change)

  5. Stable routine care, discharge, teaching

Analogy: a new neuro change is the smoke alarm; an expected symptom of a known diagnosis is the dishwasher beeping.

Informed consent

Point Rule
Who explains the procedure The provider performing it (risks, benefits, alternatives)
Nurse's role Witness the signature, confirm the client understands, verify competence
Client asks a question about risks Stop; notify the provider before signing
Client received opioid or sedative Consent not valid; obtain before premedication
Minors Parent/guardian, except emancipated minors, married, military, or specific care (STI, contraception, pregnancy, substance treatment in many states)
Emergency Implied consent when delay threatens life
Interpreter Use a trained medical interpreter, never a family member or child

Advance directives and client rights

  • Living will: states wishes about life-sustaining treatment. Health care proxy / durable POA for health care: names the person who decides when the client cannot.

  • DNR does not mean "do not treat"; comfort care, meds, and nursing care continue.

  • A competent adult may refuse any treatment, including life-saving treatment. The nurse informs the provider and documents; does not coerce.

  • Leaving AMA: notify provider, explain risks, have client sign the AMA form, document. You cannot physically stop a competent adult.

Legal and ethical terms

Term Meaning Test-question signal
Negligence Failure to act as a reasonable nurse would Omitted fall precautions
Malpractice Professional negligence causing harm; needs duty, breach, causation, damages Wrong med causing injury
Assault Threat of harmful touch "If you don't take this, I'll hold you down"
Battery Touching without consent Procedure after refusal

Autonomy Client's right to decide Respect refusal
Beneficence / Nonmaleficence Do good / do no harm

Fidelity / Veracity Keep promises / tell the truth

Confidentiality (HIPAA)

  • Share only with those directly involved in care. No client information on social media, even without names.

  • Staff member breaches confidentiality: intervene immediately, then report per policy.

Mandatory reporting

Child abuse, elder abuse, certain communicable diseases (TB, measles, pertussis, syphilis, HIV per state law), gunshot wounds, and animal bites are reported by law. Suspicion is enough; proof is not required.

Handoff and orders

  • Use SBAR: Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation.

  • Verbal/phone orders: write down, read back, verify, then document. Question any order that is unclear, unsafe, or outside parameters before carrying it out.

  • Incident reports are not placed in the chart and are not mentioned in the nurse's notes. Document the facts of the event in the chart.

2. Safety & Infection Prevention and Control (10–16%)

Most items test which isolation to use, PPE order, restraint rules, and fall/fire safety. Pick the precaution by how the organism travels, not by how sick the client is.

Transmission-based precautions

Precaution Organisms (high-yield) Room PPE
Airborne ("My Chicken Hez TB") Measles, Chickenpox/disseminated zoster, TB, (COVID-19 aerosol procedures) Private negative-pressure (AIIR), door closed N95 or PAPR; client wears surgical mask for transport
Droplet ("SPIDERMAN") Sepsis/meningitis (N. meningitidis), Pneumonia/Pertussis, Influenza, Diphtheria, Epiglottitis (Hib), Rubella, Mumps, Adenovirus, Mycoplasma Private or cohort, 3 ft+ Surgical mask within 3–6 ft; client masked for transport
Contact ("MRS WEE") MRSA/VRE/MDROs, RSV, Skin (scabies, lice, impetigo, herpes zoster localized in immunocompromised), Wounds, Enteric (C. diff, E. coli O157, rotavirus, Hep A), Eye (conjunctivitis) Private or cohort Gown + gloves; dedicated equipment (stethoscope, BP cuff)
Protective (neutropenic) ANC < 500, transplant Positive-pressure room Mask on client outside room; no fresh flowers, raw fruit/veg, standing water

C. diff: soap and water only. Alcohol rub does not kill spores. Clean with bleach-based disinfectant.

PPE order

flowchart LR
  subgraph ON[Donning]
  a1[Hand hygiene] --> a2[Gown] --> a3[Mask/N95] --> a4[Goggles/shield] --> a5[Gloves]
  end
  subgraph OFF[Doffing]
  b1[Gloves] --> b2[Goggles/shield] --> b3[Gown] --> b4[Mask/N95<br/>outside room] --> b5[Hand hygiene]
  end

Rule of thumb: remove the dirtiest item first (gloves), and the respirator last, after the door is closed. Image: CDC donning and doffing poster (PDF)

Restraints

  • Last resort after less restrictive measures fail. Requires a provider order; never PRN.

  • Order renewal: adults every 4 hr, ages 9–17 every 2 hr, under 9 every 1 hr (violent/self-destructive behavior, per CMS). Face-to-face evaluation within 1 hr for violent behavior.

  • Check circulation, skin, and neuro status every 15 min for violent restraint (per policy); release, ROM, toileting, fluids at least every 2 hr.

  • Tie to the bed frame, never the side rail. Quick-release knot. Two fingers under the restraint.

  • Four side rails up counts as a restraint.

Fall prevention

Bed low and locked, call light in reach, nonskid socks, frequent rounding and toileting, night light, bed alarm. Highest risk: older adults, sedatives/diuretics/antihypertensives, orthostatic hypotension, vertigo (Ménière), confusion.

Fire and hazards

  • RACE: Rescue, Alarm, Contain (close doors), Extinguish/Evacuate.

  • PASS: Pull pin, Aim at base, Squeeze, Sweep.

  • Evacuate ambulatory clients first, then those needing assistance, then bedbound (horizontal evacuation first). In a disaster, discharge the most stable clients first.

  • Internal radiation (sealed implant): private room, lead container and long forceps in room, limit visits to 30 min/day, visitors 6 ft away, no pregnant visitors or children. Time, distance, shielding.

  • Frayed cord or malfunctioning equipment: remove from service, tag, report.

Seizure precautions

Padded rails, bed low, suction and O₂ at bedside. During a seizure: turn to side, protect head, loosen clothing, time it, nothing in the mouth, do not restrain.

Client identification and errors

Two identifiers (name + DOB), never room number. Near misses and errors are documented in an incident report and reported; the nurse assesses the client first.

3. Health Promotion & Maintenance (6–12%)

This category holds OB, newborn, growth and development, and screening. OB content below follows your OB final (Chapters 5–23); peds follows Wong's and HESI RN Pediatrics.

Antepartum essentials

Topic Key facts
GTPAL Gravida (pregnancies, including current), Term (37+ wk), Preterm (20–36 6/7 wk), Abortions (before 20 wk), Living
Naegele's rule First day of LMP − 3 months + 7 days + 1 year
Fundal height At umbilicus at 20 wk; after that, cm ≈ weeks (±2) from 20–36 wk
Glucose screen 24–28 wk (1-hr 50 g GCT)
GBS culture 36–37 wk; positive = IV penicillin in labor
Rh immune globulin Rh− mother: 28 wk and within 72 hr after birth of Rh+ baby
Danger signs Vaginal bleeding, gush of fluid, severe headache, visual changes, epigastric pain, decreased fetal movement, fever, edema of face/hands

Watch out: GTPAL boundary is 20 weeks. A loss before 20 wk counts as A; a birth at 20–36 6/7 wk counts as P. State whether the client is currently pregnant, because that changes G.

Bleeding in pregnancy

Condition Pain Uterus Key nursing point
Placenta previa Painless bright red bleeding Soft, nontender No vaginal exams; ultrasound
Abruptio placentae Painful, dark bleeding (may be concealed) Board-like, rigid Watch for shock and DIC
Ectopic pregnancy Unilateral pelvic pain, shoulder pain if ruptured — Rupture = surgical emergency; methotrexate if unruptured
Hydatidiform mole Painless dark brown bleeding, large-for-dates, very high hCG, hyperemesis Enlarged Follow hCG for 6–12 months; no pregnancy during follow-up

Hypertensive disorders and magnesium

  • Preeclampsia: BP ≥ 140/90 after 20 wk + proteinuria or end-organ signs. Severe features: BP ≥ 160/110, headache, visual changes, RUQ/epigastric pain, low platelets, elevated liver enzymes.

  • HELLP: Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets.

  • Magnesium sulfate prevents seizures. Therapeutic level 4–7 mEq/L. Antidote: calcium gluconate.

Watch out: MgSO₄ toxicity progresses in order: loss of DTRs first, then respiratory depression (RR < 12), then cardiac arrest. Check DTRs, RR, urine output (≥ 30 mL/hr) hourly.

Watch out: Hold terbutaline if maternal HR > 120.

Fetal monitoring

Normal FHR baseline 110–160. Use VEAL CHOP:

Pattern Cause Nursing action
Variable decels Cord compression Reposition, stop oxytocin, O₂, amnioinfusion, vaginal exam for prolapse
Early decels Head compression None, benign
Accelerations OK None, reassuring
Late decels Placental insufficiency Stop oxytocin, left side, IV bolus, O₂, notify provider

Images: StatPearls: fetal heart rate changes figure · Lecturio: fetal decelerations

  • NST reactive = good (2 accels of 15 bpm × 15 sec in 20 min). Watch out: you once read "reactive" as a negative finding.

  • Modified BPP = NST + amniotic fluid. AFI < 5 cm or single deepest pocket ≤ 2 cm = oligohydramnios.

  • Watch out: Oligohydramnios links to renal agenesis/urinary obstruction and cord compression. Polyhydramnios links to GI obstruction (esophageal atresia), neural tube defects, and maternal diabetes. Analogy: the fetus drinks and pees the fluid; if it cannot swallow, fluid builds up; if it cannot pee, fluid runs low.

  • Watch out: Scalp stimulation is done after the deceleration resolves, not during it.

Intrapartum emergencies

Emergency First action
Prolapsed cord Gloved hand lifts presenting part off the cord, knee-chest or Trendelenburg, call for help; do not push cord back
Shoulder dystocia McRoberts maneuver + suprapubic pressure (never fundal pressure)
Uterine rupture Sudden sharp pain, loss of station, fetal distress: emergency C-section
Amniotic fluid embolism Sudden dyspnea, hypotension, DIC: O₂, CPR, call rapid response

Watch out (HIV in labor): avoid anything that breaks fetal skin: fetal scalp electrode, forceps, vacuum, episiotomy. Epidural and amnioinfusion are allowed.

Postpartum

  • Fundus firm, midline, at umbilicus after birth, descending ~1 cm/day. Boggy fundus: massage first, then assess bladder (full bladder displaces uterus to the right).

  • Lochia: rubra (days 1–3), serosa (4–10), alba (10+). Saturating a pad in 15 min or clots larger than an egg = hemorrhage.

  • PPH meds: oxytocin, methylergonovine (hold if hypertensive), carboprost (avoid in asthma), misoprostol, tranexamic acid.

  • Watch out: Postpartum blues (days 2–14, weepy, resolves) is a named entity to monitor. Depression = beyond 2 weeks or interferes with function. Psychosis = emergency (risk to infant).

Newborn

Item Normal / action
APGAR 5 signs (HR, respiratory effort, tone, reflex irritability, color), each 0–2, at 1 and 5 min; 7–10 normal

Watch out: Meconium has the longest drug-detection window for in-utero exposure (vs. urine or hair). NAS: Finnegan scoring, swaddle, low stimulation, small frequent feeds.

Pediatric vital signs change with age. Watch out: In HESI's key, 100°F (37.8°C) in an active 17-year-old after practice is normal, and no action is needed. Elsevier's rationales win for HESI items.

Screening and prevention

  • Colorectal cancer screening begins at 45 (current ACS). Some older question keys still say 50.

  • Mammogram: yearly from 40–45 depending on guideline. Pap: 21–65.

  • Cancer risk (test plan sample): heavy alcohol plus age is the highest-risk profile.

  • Car seats: rear-facing until at least age 2 or the seat's limit; back seat until 13.

  • Lead screening at 12 and 24 months in high-risk areas.

  • Live vaccines (MMR, varicella, intranasal flu) are avoided in pregnancy and severe immunosuppression.

4. Psychosocial Integrity (6–12%)

Safety comes first here too: suicide risk and violence risk outrank every communication technique. Content follows your NUR202 Weeks 1–3 guides (Halter/Varcarolis) plus core psych topics.

Therapeutic communication

Therapeutic (pick these) Nontherapeutic (eliminate these)
Open-ended questions: "Tell me about…" "Why" questions (sound accusatory)
Reflecting feelings: "You seem frightened." False reassurance: "Everything will be fine."
Restating, clarifying, summarizing Giving advice: "If I were you…"
Silence, presence Changing the subject
Offering self: "I'll sit with you." Approval/disapproval, "That's good"
Focusing on the client's feelings Defending staff or provider
Presenting reality: "I don't hear voices, but I understand they're real to you." Arguing with or agreeing with a delusion

Test trick: the right answer usually names the feeling and keeps the focus on the client.

Defense mechanisms

Mechanism Example
Denial "I don't have a drinking problem" after a DUI
Projection Blaming others for one's own feelings
Displacement Angry at boss, yells at spouse
Rationalization Excuses: "Everyone drinks to cope"
Reaction formation Acting the opposite of the true feeling
Regression Adult with illness acts childlike
Sublimation Channeling aggression into sports (healthy)
Suppression Consciously putting a thought aside (healthy) vs. repression (unconscious)

Legal in psych

  • Voluntary admission: client can request discharge (may be held briefly for evaluation). Involuntary: danger to self or others, or gravely disabled.

  • Clients keep their rights when admitted, including refusing medication unless an emergency or court order applies.

  • Duty to warn (Tarasoff): a specific threat against an identifiable person must be reported.

  • Seclusion and restraint: last resort, provider order, face-to-face evaluation within 1 hr.

Suicide risk

  • Ask directly: "Are you thinking of killing yourself?" Asking does not plant the idea.

  • Highest risk: a plan, access to means, previous attempt, sudden calm after deep depression (energy returns as antidepressants start working, around 10–14 days).

  • Warning signs: giving away possessions, saying goodbye, writing a will.

  • Actions: 1:1 observation, remove sharps and cords, no-harm contracts are not reliable.

Disorder quick reference

Disorder Key features Nursing priority
Major depression Anhedonia, sleep/appetite change, hopelessness ≥ 2 wk Suicide assessment, small achievable goals
Bipolar (mania) Flight of ideas, grandiosity, no sleep, pressured speech Safety, finger foods, low stimulation, short simple statements
Schizophrenia Positive: hallucinations, delusions. Negative: flat affect, alogia, avolition Reality orientation; ask about command hallucinations
GAD / panic Worry; panic = sudden terror, chest pain, feeling of doom Stay with client, calm short directions, deep breathing
PTSD Flashbacks, hypervigilance, avoidance, numbing Safe environment, trauma-informed care
Dissociative disorders Memory gaps, depersonalization, identity alteration Safety, grounding techniques
OCD Obsessions + rituals relieving anxiety Do not interrupt rituals abruptly; set limits gradually
Anorexia BMI < 18.5, fear of weight gain, amenorrhea, bradycardia Supervised meals, weigh with back to scale, watch for refeeding syndrome (low phosphate)
Bulimia Binge-purge, normal weight, Russell sign, dental erosion, hypokalemia Observe 1–2 hr after meals, monitor K⁺

Dementia vs. delirium

Delirium Dementia
Onset Sudden (hours–days) Gradual (months–years)
Course Fluctuates, worse at night Progressive
Attention Impaired Intact early
Reversible? Usually (treat cause: infection/UTI, meds, hypoxia, electrolytes) No

Analogy: delirium is a power outage from a tripped breaker (find the cause, flip it back); dementia is wiring that is slowly corroding.

Substance withdrawal

Substance Withdrawal / toxicity Treatment
Alcohol Tremors 6–12 hr, seizures 12–48 hr, delirium tremens 48–72 hr (can be fatal) Benzodiazepines (CIWA-Ar), thiamine before glucose (prevents Wernicke), seizure precautions
Opioids Withdrawal: yawning, rhinorrhea, diarrhea, goosebumps, dilated pupils (uncomfortable, rarely fatal). Overdose: pinpoint pupils, RR < 10 Overdose: naloxone. Withdrawal: methadone, buprenorphine, clonidine
Benzodiazepines Withdrawal can cause seizures Slow taper; overdose antidote flumazenil
Stimulants Crash: depression, hypersomnia, suicide risk Supportive, safety

Disulfiram: no alcohol in any form (mouthwash, cough syrup, hand sanitizer) — severe vomiting, flushing, hypotension.

Abuse, crisis, grief

  • Abuse: interview the client alone, document exact words and injuries objectively, report child/elder abuse. For intimate partner violence, safety plan; the adult decides whether to leave.

  • Crisis intervention is short-term (4–6 weeks). Priority: safety, then immediate problem-solving.

  • Grief (Kübler-Ross): denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance — not always in order. Complicated grief lasts long and impairs function.

  • Culture/spirituality: ask the client's preferences; do not assume based on religion or ethnicity.

5. Basic Care & Comfort (6–12%)

These items look easy and punish carelessness: device technique, diets, tube feeds, skin. Know the "why" behind each rule so you can reason through unfamiliar wording.

Assistive devices

Device Rule
Cane Held on the strong side; move cane and weak leg together. "Up with the good, down with the bad."
Walker Move walker first, then weak leg, then strong leg; do not carry it on stairs
Crutches 2–3 finger widths below axilla, weight on hands not axillae; stairs: up with good leg, down with crutches and bad leg
Hearing aid Turn off and remove battery at night; clean with dry cloth
Visual impairment Announce yourself, clock positions for food, client holds your arm

Mobility and immobility

  • Immobility hits every system: pressure injury, DVT, pneumonia/atelectasis, constipation, urinary stasis/calculi, contractures, bone loss.

  • Traction: weights hang freely, never on the floor or bed; do not remove weights without an order. Skeletal traction pin care per policy; watch for infection.

  • Casts: elevate, handle wet cast with palms, check 5 P's (pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis). Pain unrelieved by opioids or pain on passive stretch = compartment syndrome: notify provider immediately.

  • Hip replacement (posterior approach): no flexion > 90°, no crossing legs, no internal rotation; abduction pillow; raised toilet seat.

  • Logroll after spinal surgery.

Skin and pressure injuries

Stage Description
1 Intact skin, nonblanchable redness
2 Partial thickness, shallow open pink wound or intact blister
3 Full thickness, fat visible; no bone/tendon/muscle
4 Full thickness, bone, tendon, or muscle exposed
Unstageable Base covered by slough or eschar

Braden scale: 6–23; ≤ 18 = at risk (lower is worse). Prevention: turn every 2 hr, HOB ≤ 30° unless contraindicated, float heels, keep skin clean and dry, protein intake. Do not massage reddened areas.

Nutrition and special diets

Diet / condition Key rule
Dumping syndrome (post-gastrectomy) Small frequent meals, low simple sugar (no ice cream, sweets), high protein/fat, no fluids with meals, lie down 20–30 min after eating
Renal (CKD) Limit sodium, potassium, phosphorus, fluid; protein moderated (higher on dialysis)
Heart failure / HTN Sodium < 2 g/day, daily weight
Celiac disease No BRWO: barley, rye, wheat, oats. Rice, corn, potatoes OK
Low potassium foods Apples, berries, rice, bread vs. high-K: bananas, oranges, potatoes, tomatoes, spinach, avocado
Warfarin Keep vitamin K intake consistent (leafy greens), do not eliminate
MAOIs Avoid tyramine: aged cheese, cured meats, red wine, soy sauce
Dysphagia Upright 90°, chin tuck, thickened liquids, no straws
Pancreatitis NPO initially, then low fat
Hepatic encephalopathy Moderate protein per current guidelines, lactulose

Tube feedings

  • Watch out: Initial placement must be confirmed by x-ray (Iggy 11e). pH and auscultation alone are not acceptable as sole confirmation. Before each feed, check pH/residual and external mark per policy.

  • HOB ≥ 30–45° during and 30–60 min after feeding.

  • Flush with 30 mL water before and after meds and feeds; give meds separately.

  • Diarrhea: check rate, concentration, contamination, meds (sorbitol, antibiotics), C. diff.

  • Aspiration signs: coughing, dyspnea, drop in SpO₂: stop the feeding first.

Elimination

  • Urinary retention: bladder scan; straight cath if > 400–500 mL per protocol.

  • Incontinence types: stress (cough/laugh, Kegels), urge (overactive bladder, timed voiding, anticholinergics), overflow (retention, catheter), functional (can't reach the toilet).

  • Enemas: left side (Sims), 12 in above anus, stop if cramping.

  • Colostomy: stoma should be pink/red and moist; dusky or black = ischemia, notify. Pouch opening 1/8 in larger than stoma; empty when 1/3 full.

Pain and comfort

  • Pain is what the client says it is. Use the right scale: numeric for adults, FACES for ages 3+, FLACC for infants/nonverbal, PAINAD for dementia.

  • Nonpharmacologic: repositioning, heat/cold, distraction, guided imagery, music, massage (not over DVT).

  • Reassess 30–60 min after PO, 15–30 min after IV.

Complementary therapies (bleeding risk and interactions)

  • "4 G's" increase bleeding: ginkgo, garlic, ginger, ginseng (plus fish oil, vitamin E). Stop before surgery.

  • St. John's wort: serotonin syndrome with SSRIs; reduces effect of oral contraceptives, warfarin, digoxin.

Sleep and postmortem

  • Cluster care to protect sleep; avoid caffeine and heavy meals before bed.

  • Postmortem: close eyes, dentures in, supine with HOB slightly raised (prevents pooling in face), remove tubes unless autopsy/coroner case, respect cultural rites.

6. Pharmacological & Parenteral Therapies (13–19%)

For any drug, know four things (your ANTS framework): Action, Nursing considerations, Therapeutic use, Side effects/safety. Most questions ask what to check before giving the drug or which finding means stop.

Autonomic nervous system drugs (Lilley Ch. 18–20)

Analogy: the sympathetic system is the gas pedal (fight or flight), the parasympathetic is the brake (rest and digest). Agonists press a pedal; blockers take a foot off it.

Receptor Location Stimulation causes
Alpha-1 Arterioles, pupils, bladder neck Vasoconstriction (↑ BP), mydriasis, urinary retention
Alpha-2 Presynaptic, CNS ↓ norepinephrine release (↓ BP) — clonidine
Beta-1 ("1 heart") Heart ↑ HR, ↑ contractility
Beta-2 ("2 lungs") Lungs, uterus, liver Bronchodilation, uterine relaxation, glycogenolysis
Dopaminergic Renal, mesenteric vessels Vasodilation, ↑ renal perfusion

  • Dopamine is dose-dependent: low = dopaminergic, medium = beta-1, high = alpha-1 ("D-B-A going up").

  • Nonselective beta blockers (propranolol) cause bronchoconstriction: avoid in asthma/COPD. Cardioselective (metoprolol, atenolol) are safer.

  • Never stop beta blockers abruptly: rebound tachycardia, HTN, MI. Hold for HR < 60 or SBP < 90–100 per parameters. They mask hypoglycemia signs (tachycardia).

  • Phentolamine: alpha blocker, antidote for norepinephrine/dopamine extravasation.

  • Cholinergic crisis = SLUDGE (Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI upset, Emesis) + bradycardia, bronchospasm. Antidote: atropine (organophosphates: atropine + pralidoxime).

  • Bethanechol: urinary retention; never IV or IM. Donepezil: Alzheimer's. Pyridostigmine: myasthenia gravis. Physostigmine: antidote for anticholinergic toxicity.

  • Anticholinergic toxicity: "hot as a hare, dry as a bone, red as a beet, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter."

Perioperative

Watch out: Pre-op teaching priority is NPO status (prevents aspiration), ranked above bleeding risk. This was a prior correction.

Tubes and devices

  • NG tube for decompression: low intermittent suction; irrigate with NS only if ordered; output measured. Placement confirmed by x-ray.

  • Chest tube: gentle continuous bubbling in the suction chamber is normal; continuous bubbling in the water seal chamber = air leak. Tidaling in water seal is expected.   • SCDs and antiembolism stockings: apply in the morning before legs swell; remove daily for skin checks.

  • Post-procedure pulses: compare to baseline and the other side; a new absent pulse is a priority.

Watch out (percussion): dullness = something dense in the chest (fluid, consolidation, tumor). Hyperresonance = too much air (pneumothorax, emphysema). Analogy: knock on a full water jug versus an empty drum.

Watch out (IPF vs. asthma): Asthma is a reversible squeeze of the airways, like straws being pinched. IPF is scarring of the lung tissue itself, like a balloon turning to stiff leather; it is restrictive and does not reverse with bronchodilators.

Watch out (prone positioning): Most lung tissue sits in the back (dorsal). Lying supine collapses those alveoli under the weight of the heart and lungs. Proning reopens them and matches ventilation to perfusion, raising oxygenation.

Watch out (lung cancer complications): SVC syndrome is mechanical compression: facial/neck/arm swelling, JVD, dyspnea (oncologic emergency, HOB up). Paraneoplastic syndromes are hormones made by the tumor: SIADH (hyponatremia), hypercalcemia, Cushing syndrome.

Neurologic

  • Increased ICP: earliest sign is a change in LOC (restlessness, confusion). Late sign is Cushing's triad: rising systolic BP with widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, irregular respirations. Care: HOB 30°, head midline, avoid hip flexion, coughing, and straining; suction < 10 sec; quiet room.

  • GCS: 15 = normal; ≤ 8 = intubate. Posturing: decorticate (flexion, "toward the core") is less severe than decerebrate (extension).

  • Stroke: noncontrast CT first to rule out bleeding. Alteplase within 3–4.5 hr of last known well; BP < 185/110 before tPA. Swallow screen before any PO. Left hemisphere = aphasia, right-sided weakness, slow cautious. Right hemisphere = left neglect, impulsive, poor judgment (fall risk).

  • Spinal cord injury: injury at C3–C5 threatens the diaphragm. Autonomic dysreflexia (T6 or above): severe HTN, pounding headache, bradycardia, flushing above injury. Sit up first, then find the trigger (full bladder most common, then bowel, tight clothing).

  • Myasthenia gravis: weakness worse with activity; watch swallowing and breathing. Myasthenic crisis (too little medication) vs. cholinergic crisis (too much: SLUDGE). Both threaten the airway.

  • Multiple sclerosis: relapsing demyelination; avoid heat (worsens symptoms), fatigue management.

  • Guillain-Barré: ascending weakness after infection; monitor vital capacity, prepare for ventilation.

  • Parkinson: tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, shuffling gait; carbidopa-levodopa (avoid high-protein meals with doses).

  • Meningitis: headache, fever, nuchal rigidity, photophobia, nausea/vomiting (test plan sample), Kernig and Brudzinski signs. Bacterial meningococcal = droplet precautions; petechial rash = emergency.

  • Dementia vs. delirium: see Section 4.

Hematology, oncology, and immunity

  • Neutropenia (ANC < 500): fever is an emergency; protective precautions, no live plants, avoid raw foods per policy.

  • Thrombocytopenia: soft toothbrush, electric razor, no IM injections, no aspirin/NSAIDs.

  • Sickle cell crisis: hydration, O₂, pain control (opioids); avoid cold, high altitude, dehydration.

  • DIC: clotting and bleeding at once — low platelets and fibrinogen, high D-dimer, oozing from sites. Treat the cause; replace products.

  • Tumor lysis syndrome: high K⁺, phosphate, uric acid; low Ca²⁺. Hydration and allopurinol/rasburicase.

  • Spinal cord compression: new back pain in a cancer client = emergency.

  • HIV: CD4 < 200 = AIDS; standard precautions.

  • Lupus: butterfly rash, joint pain, kidney involvement; avoid sun, infection prevention on steroids.

  • Anaphylaxis: IM epinephrine (anterolateral thigh) before antihistamines or steroids.

  • Transplant: hyperacute rejection (minutes to hours), acute (weeks to months: fever, graft tenderness, ↓ function), chronic (months to years). Immunosuppressants raise infection risk; take them lifelong and on schedule.

Musculoskeletal

  • Compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion, pain on passive stretch; loosen dressings/cast, keep limb at heart level (not elevated), notify.

  • Fat embolism (24–72 hr after long-bone fracture): dyspnea, confusion, petechiae on chest. O₂, notify.

  • Osteoporosis: calcium + vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise. Alendronate: upright 30 min after with a full glass of water, empty stomach.

  • RA (autoimmune, symmetric, morning stiffness > 1 hr) vs. OA (wear and tear, asymmetric, pain worse with activity).

  • Gout: avoid purines (organ meats, shellfish, alcohol); allopurinol prevents, colchicine treats attacks.

  • Amputation: elevate residual limb only first 24–48 hr; then lie prone periodically to prevent hip flexion contracture.

Burns

Image: StatPearls: Rule of Nines

  • Adult rule of nines: head 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%.

  • Parkland formula: 4 mL × kg × %TBSA (partial and full thickness only) of LR over 24 hr; half in the first 8 hr from the time of the burn, the rest over 16 hr. Target urine output 0.5 mL/kg/hr (adult).

  • Inhalation injury (singed nasal hair, soot in sputum, hoarseness, stridor): airway first, anticipate early intubation.

  • Electrical burns: cardiac monitoring (dysrhythmias), hidden internal damage.

  • Emergent phase (first 24–48 hr): fluid shifts out of vessels, hyperkalemia. Acute phase: fluid returns, diuresis, infection risk.
