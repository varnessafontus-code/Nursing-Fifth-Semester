Chapter 8: Therapeutic Relationships

Personal vs. therapeutic relationship — the core distinction tested repeatedly: a personal relationship exists for mutual friendship/enjoyment; a therapeutic relationship exists for the patient’s needs, thoughts, feelings, and goals — it is not mutual in that sense, and it has a defined endpoint (termination).

Peplau’s four phases — memorize the order and what happens in each: 1. Preorientation — nurse prepares before ever meeting the patient (chart review, researching the diagnosis/meds). 2. Orientation — first meetings; establish trust, contract (purpose, time, place, confidentiality limits), assess the patient, identify problems. 3. Working — the bulk of the relationship; patient explores problems, develops coping strategies, does the actual therapeutic work. 4. Termination — planned ending; review progress, discuss feelings about ending (including the patient’s history with separation/loss), transfer of care as needed.

Core facilitative qualities: genuineness (being authentic, not playing a role), empathy (understanding and communicating understanding of the patient’s feelings — distinct from sympathy, which centers the nurse’s own feelings), positive regard (respecting the patient’s worth regardless of behavior).

Boundaries, transference, and countertransference — this is the highest-yield content in the chapter: - Transference = the patient unconsciously displaces feelings/reactions from a significant figure in their past onto the nurse. (“You’re just like my critical mother.”) - Countertransference = the nurse’s own unconscious emotional reaction to the patient, rooted in the nurse’s own past relationships. (You feel unusually protective of a patient because they remind you of your younger sibling.) - Both are normal and expected — the professional failure isn’t having the reaction, it’s failing to recognize it and letting it blur professional boundaries. Clinical supervision exists specifically to catch this. - Boundary violations (romantic/sexual relationships with patients, self-disclosure that serves the nurse rather than the patient, accepting gifts, socializing after discharge) are license-ending, not just “unprofessional.”

Meds note: minimal direct pharmacology here, but recognize that trust built in the therapeutic relationship is what makes medication teaching and adherence conversations possible later — a patient who doesn’t trust the nurse won’t disclose nonadherence or side effects honestly.

Chapter 9: Therapeutic Communication

Two-level communication: content (the actual words/verbal message) vs. process (nonverbal behavior — tone, posture, eye contact). When content and process match, communication is congruent/healthy; when they conflict (patient says “I’m fine” while crying and refusing eye contact), the message is incongruent — and the nonverbal usually carries the more accurate signal.

Factors that interfere with communication: personal (psychiatric symptoms — slowed thinking in depression, poor concentration in anxiety, distractibility in mania), environmental (noise, lack of privacy), and relationship-based (cultural mismatch, power differential).

Therapeutic techniques you must be able to identify by name (classic matching-question territory): - Silence — gives the patient time to think/collect themselves. - Restating — repeating the patient’s own words back. - Reflecting — naming the underlying feeling (“You sound like you’ve had many disappointments”). - Open-ended questions — invite elaboration (“What is an example of the stress you’re under?”). Preferred over closed-ended questions except during structured intake or when you need a specific fact. - Exploring — asking the patient to elaborate more fully on an idea or relationship. - Giving broad openings — hands direction to the patient (“Where would you like to begin?”). - Presenting reality — gently stating fact without arguing, especially useful with hallucinations/delusions (“I don’t see anyone else in the room, but I understand you do”). - Verbalizing the implied — making explicit what the patient hints at. - Seeking clarification — “I’m not sure I follow you; can you say more?”

Nontherapeutic techniques — know these as the classic wrong answers: - Excessive questioning (rapid-fire closed questions = interrogation). - Giving approval/disapproval (judgment, even positive judgment, shifts focus onto the nurse’s opinion). - Asking “why” questions (puts the patient on the defensive, demands justification). - Giving advice, false reassurance, defending a third party, changing the subject/introducing unrelated topics.

Process recording (SLO 8): a verbatim transcript of a nurse-patient interaction, annotated with (1) the communication technique used in each line and (2) the student’s own thoughts/feelings during the exchange. Purpose: it forces you to see your own patterns — where you go nontherapeutic under discomfort (classic trigger: patient expresses suicidal ideation or anger, and the untrained response is to reassure, defend, or change the subject). Expect to complete one of these for your clinical rotation; the skill you’re building is Chapter 9.

Cultural note (bridges to Ch. 5): eye contact, touch, and personal space norms vary by culture — don’t assume avoidant eye contact signals dishonesty or disengagement; it may reflect a cultural norm of respect.

Meds note: therapeutic communication is the vehicle for medication teaching and adherence assessment — a patient who feels interrogated (excessive questioning) about “why” they stopped taking their medication will shut down; open-ended exploration (“Tell me about your experience with the medication”) gets you the actual barrier (side effects, cost, stigma, forgetting).

Chapter 9 — 10 NCLEX-Style Questions

  1. A patient says, “Nobody likes me.” The nurse responds, “Give me an example of one person who doesn’t like you.” This technique is:
    1. Reflecting
    2. Exploring
    3. Giving approval
    4. Presenting reality
  2. Which nurse response is an example of a nontherapeutic technique?
    1. “Tell me more about that.”
    2. “Why did you stop taking your medication?”
    3. “You seem upset. What are you thinking about?”
    4. “I’m not sure I follow — can you explain that again?”
  3. A patient reports hearing voices that aren’t there. Which nurse response best demonstrates “presenting reality”?
    1. “There’s no such thing as voices; you’re imagining it.”
    2. “I don’t hear any voices, but I understand that you do, and it sounds frightening.”
    3. “Let’s talk about something else.”
    4. “You need to ignore the voices and they’ll go away.”
  4. A patient says, “I can’t talk to you or anyone else. It’s a waste of time.” The nurse replies, “Do you feel that no one understands?” This is an example of:
    1. Verbalizing the implied
    2. Giving broad openings
    3. Voicing doubt
    4. Excessive questioning
  5. Which of the following is an example of a closed-ended question appropriately used during an intake assessment?
    1. “What is your biggest concern right now?”
    2. “How would you describe your relationship with your spouse?”
    3. “When did you last take your medication?”
    4. “Tell me about your typical day.”
  6. A patient’s verbal statement is “I’m not angry,” but the patient is clenching fists, raising their voice, and avoiding eye contact. The nurse recognizes this as:
    1. Congruent communication
    2. Incongruent communication between content and process
    3. Therapeutic use of silence
    4. A culturally appropriate communication style
  7. Which of the following is the primary purpose of a process recording assignment?
    1. To document a patient’s medication administration record
    2. To help the student identify their own communication patterns, including therapeutic and nontherapeutic responses
    3. To replace the official nursing progress note
    4. To evaluate the patient’s competency for discharge
  8. A student writes in a process recording: “The patient said he wanted to die, and I told him everything would be fine and changed the subject.” Reviewing this, the student should identify this response as:
    1. Therapeutic use of reassurance
    2. Nontherapeutic — false reassurance and introducing an unrelated topic
    3. An appropriate way to redirect a suicidal patient
    4. Reflective listening
  9. A patient from a culture where direct eye contact with an authority figure is considered disrespectful avoids eye contact with the nurse. The nurse should interpret this as:
    1. A sign of dishonesty
    2. A possible cultural norm rather than evidence of psychopathology
    3. Definitive evidence of depression
    4. A boundary violation
  10. Which technique is being used when the nurse says, “Where would you like to begin today?”
    1. Closed-ended questioning
    2. Giving broad openings
    3. Voicing doubt
    4. Giving approval

Answer Key & Rationales — Chapter 9 1. b — Asking for a specific example to fully examine a vague statement = exploring. 2. b — “Why” questions put patients on the defensive; this is a classic nontherapeutic pattern. 3. b — States the nurse’s own perception as fact without arguing or dismissing the patient’s experience. 4. a — Making explicit what the patient only hinted at (“waste of time” → implied belief that no one understands) = verbalizing the implied. 5. c — Specific, fact-seeking question appropriate for intake; the others are open-ended and better used to build rapport/explore. 6. b — Words say one thing, behavior says another = incongruent communication; nonverbal usually carries the truer signal. 7. b — Self-awareness of communication pattern is the entire point of the assignment. 8. b — False reassurance + topic change when a patient discloses suicidal ideation is a textbook nontherapeutic (and clinically risky) response — this should trigger direct follow-up and safety assessment instead. 9. b — Don’t pathologize culturally normative behavior. 10. b — Hands direction of the conversation to the patient without imposing a specific topic = giving broad openings.

Chapter 5: Cultural Implications

Culture shapes everything about how distress is experienced, expressed, and treated — this is the connective tissue between Ch. 1’s continuum concept and today’s assessment skills.

Key vocabulary (DSM-5 terminology, replaced the old “culture-bound syndrome” language): - Cultural concepts of distress — the umbrella term, with three subtypes: - Cultural idioms of distress — phrases specific to a culture that express suffering (“nervous wreck,” “heavy heart”). - Cultural explanations/perceived causes — culturally specific beliefs about why the illness occurred (e.g., historically, demonic possession as an explanation for seizures or OCD symptoms). - Cultural syndromes — recognized patterns within a specific culture (e.g., ataque de nervios among Hispanic populations — trembling, palpitations, dissociative-like episodes triggered by acute stress/loss, with little memory of the episode afterward).

Cultural competence = five constructs (Campinha-Bacote model) — a classic matching set: 1. Cultural awareness — recognizing that you and your patient both have a culture. 2. Cultural knowledge — actively seeking information about other cultures. 3. Cultural encounters — direct interactions that build skill and prevent stereotyping (individual members of any culture express norms differently — don’t assume uniformity). 4. Cultural skill — the ability to actually perform a culturally sensitive assessment. 5. Cultural desire — genuine motivation to provide culturally responsive care (not compliance — desire).

Kleinman’s cultural assessment questions (useful, practical, testable): “What do you call this illness?” “What do you think caused it?” “How does it work/what does it do to you?” “How long will it last?” “How do you think it should be treated?” These operationalize a culturally humble assessment far better than a checklist.

Barriers to culturally competent care: - Stigma — often more severe in cultures emphasizing family interdependence/honor, where illness is seen as reflecting on the whole family, driving concealment and delayed care. - Misdiagnosis — many standardized assessment tools were validated on primarily White populations and may not translate across cultures. - Somatization — expressing psychological distress as physical symptoms (back pain, fatigue, dizziness) rather than naming an emotional cause. More common in cultures where mind/body are viewed as one, or where mental health carries heavy stigma. - Pharmacogenetics — genetic variation across ethnic groups affects drug metabolism (e.g., variability in cytochrome P450 enzyme activity), meaning standard dosing may not be equally effective/safe across all populations. This is a direct bridge into Ch. 3’s pharmacology content — cultural competence isn’t just about communication style, it has real pharmacokinetic implications. - Interpreters vs. family members — reiterated here: professional interpreters are required for accurate, unbiased communication.

Meds note: always assess a patient’s cultural beliefs about medication (some cultures view psychiatric medication with more stigma than psychotherapy, or prefer traditional/herbal remedies) — nonadherence rooted in cultural belief is a different clinical problem than nonadherence from cost or side effects, and requires a different intervention.

Chapter 6: Legal and Ethical Considerations

Six core ethical principles (know examples for each — these get tested as scenario-matching): 1. Autonomy — right to self-determination (e.g., honoring a patient’s right to refuse medication). 2. Beneficence — duty to actively benefit the patient (spending extra time calming an anxious patient). 3. Nonmaleficence — “do no harm” (protecting confidential information). 4. Justice — fair, equal distribution of care regardless of patient characteristics. 5. Fidelity — loyalty/faithfulness to the patient, maintaining competence. 6. Veracity — truthfulness (accurately describing medication side effects, not minimizing them).

Admission types: - Voluntary admission — patient applies in writing, understands the need for treatment; retains the right to request release (though the facility can seek involuntary commitment if release is deemed unsafe upon reevaluation). - Involuntary commitment — court-ordered, without patient consent. Criteria (any one is generally sufficient): diagnosed mental illness, danger to self/others, gravely disabled (can’t provide basic self-care), or in need of treatment that the illness itself prevents the person from seeking voluntarily. Most states cap the initial hold around 72 hours before a formal hearing is required.

Patients’ rights under the law: - Right to treatment (including least restrictive environment, freedom from unnecessary medication, right to an attorney). - Right to refuse treatment — patients can withhold or withdraw consent, even while involuntarily committed. This right can be overridden, but only under specific, narrow criteria. - Right to informed consent — patient must understand risks, benefits, and alternatives, and consent voluntarily (Canterbury v. Spence establishes the legal foundation: every competent adult has the right to determine what happens to their own body).

Forced/emergency medication — the single most testable legal concept in this chapter: - True emergency (imminent danger to self/others): a patient can be medicated without a court hearing. - Non-emergency override of refusal requires all of the following: 1. Serious mental illness is present. 2. The patient’s functioning is deteriorating, or they are suffering or exhibiting threatening behavior. 3. Benefits of treatment outweigh the harms. 4. The patient lacks capacity to make a reasoned treatment decision. 5. Less-restrictive alternatives have already been tried/found inadequate. - Miss any one of these five, and forced medication is not legally justified outside a true emergency. This is a “select all that apply” favorite.

Torts relevant to psychiatric nursing: - Assault — creating fear of imminent harmful contact (e.g., threatening to restrain a patient). - Battery — actual unauthorized harmful/offensive touching (e.g., administering an injection without consent, outside an emergency). - False imprisonment — unjustified restriction of a patient’s freedom of movement without legal authority. - Negligence/malpractice — failure to meet the standard of care, causing harm.

Confidentiality and duty to warn/protect: HIPAA protects patient information, but confidentiality is not absolute — nurses have a duty to warn/protect identifiable, intended victims when a patient makes a credible, specific threat (Tarasoff principle, generally discussed alongside this chapter), and mandatory reporting applies to suspected child/elder abuse regardless of confidentiality.

Documentation as legal protection: the medical record is presumed accurate/truthful in court. “If it wasn’t documented, it wasn’t done” is the operating legal assumption — chart facts and direct observations, avoid unsupported opinions or blame-laden language.

Meds note (the direct crossover with Chapter 3/16’s pharmacology): the right to refuse psychotropic medication is one of the most litigated issues in psychiatric law. When a question describes a competent, non-emergent patient refusing medication, the default answer is to respect autonomy and the right to refuse — not to medicate anyway. Overriding refusal requires the full five-criteria pathway above, or a genuine emergency.

Chapter 6 — 10 NCLEX-Style Questions

  1. A nurse spends extra time calming a highly anxious patient before a procedure. This nursing action best exemplifies which ethical principle?
    1. Justice
    2. Beneficence
    3. Veracity
    4. Fidelity
  2. Which of the following is required for involuntary commitment in most states?
    1. The patient must have committed a crime
    2. The patient must be diagnosed with mental illness AND pose danger to self/others, be gravely disabled, or be unable to voluntarily seek needed treatment
    3. The patient’s family must formally request it in writing with no clinical criteria needed
    4. The patient must be over the age of 65
  3. A voluntarily admitted patient requests release. The nurse understands that:
    1. The patient has an absolute, unconditional right to immediate release with no further evaluation
    2. The patient has the right to request release, though reevaluation may occur and could result in a shift to involuntary commitment if unsafe
    3. Voluntary patients can never be released
    4. Only a judge can authorize any release for a voluntary patient
  4. Which situation permits a healthcare team to medicate a patient against their will WITHOUT first obtaining a court hearing?
    1. The patient calmly states they don’t like the taste of the medication
    2. The patient poses an imminent, serious danger to self or others (true emergency)
    3. The patient missed one outpatient appointment
    4. The nurse personally disagrees with the patient’s choice
  5. All of the following criteria must be met to medicate a non-emergent, refusing patient after a court hearing EXCEPT:
    1. The patient has a serious mental illness
    2. Less-restrictive alternatives have been tried and found inadequate
    3. The patient’s family prefers medication over therapy
    4. The patient lacks capacity to make a reasoned treatment decision
  6. A nurse threatens to restrain a patient who has done nothing to warrant it, causing the patient to fear imminent harmful contact. This could constitute:
    1. Battery
    2. Assault
    3. Negligence
    4. Justice violation
  7. A nurse administers an injection to a competent, non-emergent patient who has clearly and repeatedly refused it. This action could constitute:
    1. Assault only
    2. Battery
    3. A justified exercise of beneficence
    4. Standard nursing practice requiring no further scrutiny
  8. A patient discloses a specific, credible plan to harm an identifiable third party. The nurse’s understanding of confidentiality in this situation is:
    1. Confidentiality is absolute; nothing can be disclosed
    2. The duty to warn/protect the identifiable victim may override standard confidentiality
    3. Only the patient’s family can be told
    4. The information can be shared with anyone who asks
  9. Which of the following best reflects the ethical principle of veracity?
    1. Distributing resources equally among all patients
    2. Truthfully describing a medication’s side effects rather than minimizing them
    3. Refusing to breach confidentiality under any circumstance
    4. Advocating for a patient’s right to refuse treatment
  10. Why is the medical record considered a strong form of legal evidence?
    1. It is written exclusively by physicians
    2. It is presumed to be accurate, honest, and made in the normal course of care
    3. It cannot be used in court under any circumstances
    4. It only reflects the patient’s subjective statements

Answer Key & Rationales — Chapter 6 1. b — Actively acting to benefit/promote well-being = beneficence. 2. b — Diagnosed mental illness PLUS at least one of the additional criteria (danger, grave disability, inability to voluntarily seek help). 3. b — Voluntary status includes the right to request release, but reevaluation can occur and may shift status if unsafe. 4. b — True imminent emergency is the only situation allowing medication without a prior court hearing. 5. c — Family preference is NOT one of the five legal criteria; the criteria center on the patient’s own clinical status and capacity, not family wishes. 6. b — Fear of imminent harmful contact without actual touching = assault (battery requires actual unauthorized contact). 7. b — Unauthorized, unconsented physical contact (the injection itself) = battery. 8. b — Duty to warn/protect can override standard confidentiality for specific, credible, identifiable threats. 9. b — Truthful communication, even when uncomfortable, is veracity. 10. b — Courts treat the record as reliable evidence precisely because it’s presumed accurate and made contemporaneously in the course of care.
