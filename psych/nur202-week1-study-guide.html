Chapter 1: Mental Health and Mental Illness

Core distinction: Mental health isn’t the absence of illness — it’s a state where a person realizes their own potential, copes with normal life stress, works productively, and contributes to their community (WHO). Mental illness = a definable psychiatric disorder (DSM-5) causing dysfunction in thinking, emotion, or behavior.

The mental health continuum — this is the concept examiners love because it kills the “healthy vs. sick” binary: - One end: well-being, full functioning, transient stress that resolves with normal coping (sleep, exercise, support, time). - Middle: mild-moderate impairment — insomnia, poor concentration, appetite change — that may or may not need professional help. - Far end: moderate-to-disabling impairment, altered thinking/mood/behavior, typically chronic. - Everyone moves along this continuum over a lifetime; it’s not a permanent label.

Diathesis-stress model: biological/genetic vulnerability (diathesis) + an environmental trigger = onset of illness. This is the standard explanation for “why did this stressful event cause a breakdown in this person and not another.” Useful lens for every clinical chapter that follows.

Epidemiology (SAMHSA 2019, know these numbers cold): - ~1 in 5 (21%) of US adults had a diagnosable mental illness in the past year. - ~5.2% had a serious mental illness (schizophrenia, bipolar, severe MDD). - Young adults 18–25 have the highest prevalence (~24%). - Comorbidity = two or more disorders present simultaneously or in sequence — expect this on a patient with, say, MDD and an anxiety disorder.

Culture shapes the definition of illness. What counts as “abnormal” is not fixed — it’s relative to the culture doing the judging. Historical example: nonconformists like Lincoln or Gandhi could have been mislabeled mentally ill under a “deviance = illness” definition. Culture-bound presentations (e.g., “running amok”) show that the behavioral expression of distress is culturally shaped even when biology is universal. This threads directly into Chapter 5.

Policy matters clinically, not just academically. The ACA (2010) expanded mental health coverage: eliminated preexisting-condition denials, made mental health/addiction treatment an essential benefit with no lifetime caps, and let young adults stay on parental insurance to age 26 (clinically relevant because most psychiatric disorders emerge in adolescence/early 20s). Know that parity laws historically applied unevenly (e.g., short-term insurance plans can be exempt).

DSM-5 classifies disorders, not people. Say “a patient with schizophrenia,” never “a schizophrenic.” This isn’t just politeness — it’s a testable communication principle.

Nursing roles: PMH-RN (generalist — assessment, milieu management, med administration, therapeutic communication) vs. PMH-APRN (advanced practice — prescribes, provides psychotherapy).

Think about it (not for credit, for you): If mental illness is partly defined by what a culture considers deviant, how do you assess a patient fairly when your cultural definition of “normal” differs from theirs? Hold that question — Chapter 5 forces you to answer it.

Chapter 1 — 10 NCLEX-Style Questions

  1. A nurse explains the mental health continuum to a nursing student. Which statement by the student indicates correct understanding?
    1. “A person is either mentally healthy or mentally ill; there’s no in-between.”

    3. “The patient in room 4 is a psych patient.”
    4. “The patient in room 4 is noncompliant.”
  4. A nurse is aware that approximately what percentage of American adults experience a serious mental illness (SMI) in a given year?
    1. 1%
    2. 5%
    3. 21%
    4. 50%
  5. Which age group has the highest 12-month prevalence of mental illness according to SAMHSA data?
    1. Adults 65 and older
    2. Adults 45–64
    3. Young adults 18–25
    4. Adolescents 12–17
  6. A patient says, “In my culture, we don’t talk about feeling sad — we say our heart is heavy.” The nurse recognizes this as an example of:
    1. A defense mechanism
    2. A cultural idiom of distress
    3. A sign of psychosis

  7. Which provision of the Affordable Care Act (2010) has the greatest direct impact on access to psychiatric care for young adults?
    1. Elimination of employer-sponsored insurance

    4. Requiring psychiatric evaluation before any primary care visit
  8. A patient tells the nurse, “I’ve been under a lot of stress at work, having trouble sleeping, and I’ve lost my appetite, but I’m still going to work every day and managing my responsibilities.” Where would the nurse place this patient on the mental health continuum?
    1. Mental health/well-being with no impairment
    2. Mild-to-moderate impairment
    3. Severe, chronic mental illness

  9. What is the primary difference in scope of practice between a PMH-RN and a PMH-APRN?

  10. A student nurse states, “Since mental illness is partly defined by cultural norms, what one culture calls a disorder, another might not recognize at all.” This statement demonstrates understanding of which concept?

    4. Epidemiology

Answer Key & Rationales — Chapter 1 1. b — The continuum is dynamic, not a fixed label (rules out a, c, d). 2. b — Diathesis (vulnerability) + stress (trigger) = onset; this is the model’s entire point. 3. b — Person-first language; the diagnosis is the illness, not the identity. 4. b — ~5.2% have SMI; ~21% have any mental illness (don’t mix these two stats up — it’s a common trap). 5. c — 18–25 year-olds show the highest prevalence (~24%). 6. b — A culturally specific phrase used to express psychological distress = cultural idiom of distress. 7. b — Direct, clinically relevant provision; most psychiatric disorders emerge in adolescence/young adulthood, so extended coverage matters. 8. b — Impaired sleep/appetite with preserved functioning = mild-to-moderate impairment, not severe illness or full wellness. 9. b — Prescriptive authority and psychotherapy are APRN-level; both levels can do MSEs and administer meds (RN admin, APRN prescribes). 10. c — This is the direct statement of cultural relativity in defining illness.

Chapter 2: Theories and Therapies

You need to know who said what and what therapy it produced — this chapter is matching-heavy on exams.

Psychoanalytic (Freud): - Levels of awareness: conscious, preconscious, unconscious (iceberg — most of the mind is below the surface, out of awareness). - Personality structure: id (primitive drives), ego (reality-based mediator), superego (internalized morals/conscience). - Defense mechanisms operate unconsciously to manage anxiety. - Transference/countertransference are Freudian concepts you’ll use for the rest of your career (see Ch. 8). - Therapy: psychodynamic therapy — free association, dream analysis, here-and-now focus; best for the “worried well,” not for psychosis or severe personality disorders.

Interpersonal (Sullivan → Peplau): - Sullivan: humans are driven by need for interaction; loneliness = the most painful human condition; anxiety arises from social insecurity. - Peplau took this and built the foundational theory of psychiatric nursing itself — the nurse-patient relationship as the core tool of practice (full detail in Ch. 8). - Interpersonal therapy targets: grief/loss, interpersonal disputes, role transitions. Effective for MDD.

Behavioral (Pavlov, Skinner): - Classical conditioning (Pavlov) = involuntary response to a paired stimulus. - Operant conditioning (Skinner) = voluntary behavior shaped by reinforcement/punishment. - Core premise: change the behavior, and you change the outcome — you don’t need to excavate childhood to get results.

Cognitive (Beck) → CBT: - Premise: thoughts precede feelings and behavior; distorted thoughts (cognitive distortions — all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralization, labeling, mental filtering) drive dysfunction. - CBT = most empirically validated psychotherapy in the book. Know it cold — it recurs in nearly every clinical chapter. - DBT (Linehan) = CBT-derived, built for chronic suicidality/self-harm and emotional dysregulation (classically borderline personality disorder). Four skill modules: mindfulness, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness, emotion regulation. - TF-CBT = trauma-specific CBT variant, short-term (12–16 sessions), includes caregivers, builds a trauma narrative gradually.

Humanistic (Maslow): - Reaction against the “pathology-focused” behavioral/psychoanalytic schools. - Hierarchy of needs: physiological → safety → love/belonging → esteem → self-actualization. Basic needs must be met before higher ones — this is why you address a psychotic patient’s safety and physical needs before doing deep psychotherapy work.

Developmental (Erikson, Kohlberg, Gilligan): - Erikson expanded Freud’s stages across the entire lifespan (psychosocial stages) — useful for identifying “stuck” developmental tasks in patients of any age. - Kohlberg: moral development from concrete/self-interested to principled reasoning. - Gilligan: critiqued Kohlberg for underweighting relationship-based moral reasoning (more common in women in his original research), emphasizing care/connection.

Meds note: none assigned; Ch. 3 (Week 3) covers the neurobiological/pharmacological counterpart to these psychological theories.

  7. According to Maslow’s hierarchy, which nursing action should take priority for a newly admitted, actively psychotic and dehydrated patient?
    1. Beginning insight-oriented group therapy
    2. Addressing hydration and physiological safety needs first
    3. Exploring the patient’s sense of self-actualization
    4. Discussing the patient’s long-term career goals
  8. Which statement reflects Erikson’s contribution beyond Freud’s original theory?
    1. He focused only on unconscious drives.

    4. He focused exclusively on moral reasoning.
  9. A nurse notices she feels unusually irritated every time she cares for a particular patient and realizes it’s because the patient reminds her of a difficult family member. This is an example of:
    1. Transference
    2. Countertransference
    3. Positive regard
    4. Sublimation
  10. Trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy (TF-CBT) is best described as:
    1. A long-term, insight-oriented therapy lasting years
    2. A short-term therapy that combines trauma-sensitive interventions with CBT and involves caregivers
    3. A purely pharmacological approach to trauma
    4. A therapy used exclusively with adults

Answer Key & Rationales — Chapter 2 1. b — Patient displacing feelings about a past figure onto the therapist/nurse = transference. 2. b — Psychodynamic therapy requires insight, motivation, and relative stability; psychosis/mania/severe PD are contraindications. 3. c — Directly targets Sullivan’s core concept of loneliness/interpersonal anxiety. 4. b — Involuntary, generalized fear response to a paired stimulus = classical conditioning. 5. b — A single bad outcome generalized to “everything” = overgeneralization (distinct from labeling, which would be “I am a failure,” and all-or-nothing, which is binary thinking without generalizing across time). 6. b — DBT was built by Linehan specifically for chronic suicidality/self-harm and emotion dysregulation. 7. b — Physiological/safety needs must be met before higher-order interventions (Maslow). 8. b — Erikson’s signature contribution is lifespan extension of developmental stages. 9. b — The nurse’s own reaction based on her own past relationship = countertransference. 10. b — Short-term (12–16 sessions), trauma-sensitive, includes caregivers.

Chapter 4: Treatment Settings

Continuum of care — know the order of restrictiveness and the settings at each level: - Inpatient (most restrictive): crisis stabilization units, acute psychiatric units, state hospitals. 24-hour monitoring, highest acuity. - Partial Hospitalization Program (PHP): ~5 days/week, ~6 hours/day. Step-down from inpatient or step-up from outpatient. Medication management available but not medically monitored/managed the way inpatient is. - Intensive Outpatient Program (IOP): 3 days/week, ~3 hours/day. Less intensive than PHP; nonresidential. - Outpatient treatment (least restrictive): individual/group sessions matched to patient need. - Also know: Assertive Community Treatment (ACT) teams — intensive case management, 24/7 support, for patients who won’t/can’t engage with traditional treatment; halfway houses — substance-free communal living, residents work outside; telepsychiatry — expanded post-COVID.

Least restrictive environment is a legal and ethical principle, not just a clinical preference — patients have a right to be treated in the least restrictive setting appropriate to their needs. This connects directly to Ch. 6 (legal/ethical) and will absolutely be tested as an independent concept.

Prevention levels (classic public-health framework, always testable): - Primary prevention: stop the disorder before it starts (education, reducing risk factors). - Secondary prevention: early detection/screening and prompt treatment to limit progression. - Tertiary prevention: minimize disability/complications once illness is established; rehabilitation-focused.

Therapeutic milieu: the structured environment itself is treatment — safety, structure, predictable routine, peer interaction, and staff modeling therapeutic behavior. Inpatient psychiatric nursing = milieu management as a core skill.

Interdisciplinary team & treatment planning: nurse is often the constant presence and frequently leads care-plan meetings because nursing is on the unit 24/7. Patient/family input into the treatment plan improves outcome success — this is testable as “which action best reflects patient-centered care.”

Meds note: Medication management availability differs sharply by setting — inpatient has 24-hour monitored administration; PHP/IOP have medication management but it’s not closely monitored; outpatient relies on patient self-administration and adherence. When a question describes a patient who’s unstable on meds and needs close monitoring, that’s your cue toward inpatient or at minimum PHP, not outpatient.

Chapter 7: The Nursing Process and Standards of Care

Six-step nursing process (ANA, since 1991 outcome ID became distinct): 1. Assessment — subjective + objective data; MSE, psychosocial assessment, history, standardized rating scales; patient is the primary source, family/police/records are secondary. 2. Diagnosis — nursing diagnosis = clinical judgment about the patient’s response to actual/potential problems (NANDA-I or ICNP framework). Structure: problem + probable cause + supporting data (“related to… as evidenced by…”). 3. Outcomes identification — measurable, positively stated, patient-centered, time-framed. 4. Planning — safe, evidence-based, culturally relevant interventions; documented in recognized terminology. 5. Implementation — basic-level interventions (coordination of care, health teaching, milieu therapy, med administration) vs. advanced-practice interventions (prescriptive authority, psychotherapy, consultation). 6. Evaluation — ongoing determination of whether outcomes were met; drives revision of the plan if not. - Documentation is sometimes called the “seventh step.” Medical records are legal documents. Chart facts, observations, and direct quotes — never unsupported opinions. Two major formats: narrative charting vs. problem-oriented (SOAPIE).

Mental Status Examination (MSE) domains you’ll be expected to assess and document: appearance, behavior/psychomotor activity, speech, mood/affect, thought process/content, perception, cognition (orientation, memory, attention), insight, and judgment.

Psychosocial assessment adds: cultural/spiritual beliefs, support system, coping style, and health beliefs/practices — this directly operationalizes SLO 1 and SLO 4 for you.

Interpreters vs. translators: an interpreter converts spoken language in real time (required by law for patients with limited English proficiency or who use ASL); a translator converts written materials. Don’t confuse the two on an exam — using a family member as an interpreter is a classic wrong answer.

Standards of Practice (ANA, APNA, ISPN): define scope, accountability, and provide the legal definition of psychiatric-mental health nursing; underpin certification and NCLEX-RN content.

Meds note: pharmacological, biological, and integrative therapies are explicitly listed as a category of implementation in the nursing process — meaning medication administration and monitoring is a nursing-process-driven activity, not something that happens outside the framework. Evaluation of medication effectiveness/side effects loops back into reassessment.

  1. Place the six steps of the nursing process in correct order.
    1. Diagnosis, Assessment, Planning, Outcomes, Implementation, Evaluation
    2. Assessment, Diagnosis, Outcomes Identification, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation

  3. Which nursing outcome statement is written correctly?

  5. Which of the following is an example of an advanced-practice-level nursing intervention?

    2. Providing individual psychotherapy
    3. Conducting a mental status examination
    4. Documenting a patient’s response to milieu therapy
  6. A nurse writes: “Patient states, ‘I haven’t slept in three nights,’ appears fatigued with dark circles under eyes, yawns repeatedly during interview.” This is an example of appropriate documentation because it:

  7. During evaluation, the nurse determines that a patient’s outcome of “will attend all group therapy sessions this week” was only partially met. The appropriate next nursing action is to:
    1. Discharge the patient regardless

  8. Which finding would the nurse document under “thought process” rather than “thought content” during a mental status exam?
    1. A patient’s stated delusion that the government is monitoring them

    4. A patient’s stated hallucination of hearing voices
  9. Why is medication administration considered part of the “implementation” step of the nursing process rather than a stand-alone task?
    1. Because medications are unrelated to nursing diagnoses
    2. Because it is a planned, evidence-based intervention tied to assessed needs and evaluated outcomes
    3. Because only physicians are responsible for medication outcomes
    4. Because implementation only includes psychotherapy
  10. A patient’s medical record will most likely be used as evidence in which of the following situations? Select the best answer.
    1. Only in cases of nurse termination
    2. In legal proceedings such as competency hearings, personal injury claims, and involuntary commitment procedures
    3. Only when the patient requests a copy
    4. Medical records are never used outside of the hospital

Answer Key & Rationales — Chapter 7 1. b — Assessment, Diagnosis, Outcomes Identification, Planning, Implementation, Evaluation. 2. b — Definitional answer directly from ANA. 3. b — Measurable, specific, patient-centered, time-framed; the others are vague, nurse-centered, or unmeasurable. 4. b — Legally required; family members risk bias, missed nuance, and confidentiality violations. 5. b — Psychotherapy, prescriptive authority, and consultation are APRN-level; the others are basic-level RN interventions. 6. b — Direct quote (subjective) + observed signs (objective), no unsupported opinion — textbook-correct documentation. 7. b — Partially met outcomes trigger reassessment and plan revision, not automatic discharge or plan abandonment. 8. c — Disorganized/tangential speech = process (the how of thinking); delusions/hallucinations/suicidal ideation = content (the what). 9. b — Implementation is tied to the whole nursing-process loop — assessed need, planned intervention, evaluated result — not an isolated task. 10. b — Medical records are legal documents used across a wide range of proceedings, not just internal HR matters.
