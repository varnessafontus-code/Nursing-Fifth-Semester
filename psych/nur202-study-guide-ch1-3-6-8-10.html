Chapter 1

Mental Health and Mental Illness

What counts as healthy, what shapes risk, how we count and classify disorders, and what psychiatric nurses do at each level of practice.

Defining mental health and mental illness

  • Health (WHO): "a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease." There is no overall health without mental health.
  • Mental health (WHO): a state of well-being in which people reach their potential, cope with normal stresses, work productively, and contribute to the community. It gives the capacity for rational thinking, communication, learning, emotional growth, resilience, and self-esteem.
  • Mental illness: all psychiatric disorders with definable diagnoses, showing significant dysfunction in thinking (Alzheimer's disease), emotion (major depressive disorder), or behavior (schizophrenia), or a mix.
  • Old definitions based on "strange" or nonconforming behavior are rejected. By that standard, Lincoln, Gandhi and Nightingale could have been called ill. Mental health is not conformity.
  • Attributes of mentally healthy people include rational thinking, meaningful relationships, effective coping and communication, resiliency, self-control, positive self-concept, self-awareness, self-care, happiness, and spiritual satisfaction.
Simply put

Mental health doesn't mean you're always happy. It means you can handle normal bad days, get your work done, get along with people, and bounce back. Mental illness is when the brain's thinking, feelings, or behavior stop working well enough that daily life gets hard, and it lasts.

How common it is (2019)

  • About 1 in 5 U.S. adults (21%) had a mental illness
  • Ages 18–25 had the highest rate, about 24%
  • About 5.2% lived with a serious mental illness
  • Only about 16% of adults received treatment

Mental vs. physical illness

The textbook calls this split artificial: the brain is an organ, so mental illness is physical illness. Treating it as "all in the head" or a personal choice feeds stigma, the belief that the whole person is flawed, shown through shunning, disgrace, and shame.

The mental health–mental illness continuum

Health and illness are endpoints on a line, not an either/or. Everyone sits somewhere on it and can shift over time, and people at the far end can move back through recovery.

Well-beingOccasional stress to mild distressNo impairment
Emotional problems or concernsMild to moderate distressMild or temporary impairment (insomnia, poor concentration, low appetite)
Mental illnessMarked distressModerate to disabling or chronic impairment
Simply put

Think of a dimmer switch, not an on/off switch. A failed exam might dim you for a day or two. Mild depression or ADHD sits in the middle. Schizophrenia or severe depression is dimmed low for a long time. The key difference at the illness end is long-term impairment, not just feeling bad.

Resilience

  • Resilience is the ability and capacity to secure the resources you need to support your well-being. Example: children of poverty or abuse who seek out trusted adults.
  • Resilient people are still affected by stress. They regulate their emotions and avoid dwelling on negative, self-defeating thoughts.
  • Brief Resilient Coping Scale: 4 items, total 4–20. Scores 4–13 are low resilient copers, 14–16 medium, 17–20 high.
Simply put

Resilience is like a phone that goes looking for a charger when the battery is low. The battery still drains; the resilient person goes and finds the outlet (a new job, a support person, a plan).

Culture and mental illness

  • There is no single standard for mental health because it is partly culturally defined. One view: mental illness is behavior a culture sees as violating its norms.
  • Definitions change with the times. "Hysteria" was once a diagnosis for women and faded as women's rights improved.
  • Culture-bound syndromes occur in specific cultures and are easily recognized there. Example: running amok in parts of Southeast Asia (usually a man, sudden near-indiscriminate violence). Anorexia nervosa has been recognized mainly in the U.S., Europe and Australia; social media may be spreading it.
  • Hearing God's voice can be desired in some religious groups but read as a symptom in others, which is why culture makes diagnosis harder.

Nature, nurture, and the diathesis-stress model

  • History swung from supernatural explanations, to germ theory (dropped because no "mania germ" existed), to psychological theories, and back toward biology after chlorpromazine (Thorazine) in 1952 calmed agitated patients.
  • The diathesis-stress model is the most accepted explanation: diathesis = biological or genetic predisposition; stress = environmental stress or trauma. Most disorders come from both together.
Simply put

Genes load the gun and stress pulls the trigger. Some people have a lot of genetic "loading" and get sick with a small push. Others have little loading but get sick after a huge push from life.

Recovery and the consumer movement

  • People with mental illness ("consumers") pushed back against forced, paternalistic treatment. NAMI (National Alliance on Mental Illness) formed in 1979.
  • Recovery (SAMHSA): "a process of change through which individuals improve their health and wellness, live a self-directed life, and strive to reach their full potential." The focus shifts from compliance to self-determination.

SAMHSA's 10 guiding principles of recovery

  • Emerges from hope (the catalyst)
  • Is person-driven (self-determination)
  • Occurs through many pathways and is nonlinear, with setbacks
  • Is holistic: mind, body, spirit, community
  • Is supported by peers and allies
  • Is supported through relationships and social networks
  • Is culturally based and influenced
  • Is supported by addressing trauma (trauma-informed care)
  • Involves individual, family and community strengths and responsibility
  • Is based on respect

Classifying disorders: DSM-5 and nursing diagnoses

  • DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013) gives the diagnostic criteria for about 157 disorders. It is the dominant system in the U.S. and also collects epidemiological statistics.
  • The DSM classifies disorders, not people. Use person-first language: "a person with schizophrenia," never "a schizophrenic."
  • ICD-10-CM is the international classification (WHO), adapted for the U.S. Both are used for care planning and reimbursement.
  • Nursing care is based on responses to illness. RNs don't diagnose or treat major depressive disorder; they treat the insomnia, hopelessness, or self-care deficit it causes.
  • Your 9th edition uses the International Classification for Nursing Practice (ICNP) for standardized nursing diagnoses.
Simply put

The DSM-5 answers "what disorder is this?" and guides medical treatment. The nursing diagnosis answers "what problem is this person having because of it, and what can the nurse do?"

Exam trap
  • Older questions call the DSM-5 "multiaxial." It isn't; the DSM-5 dropped the multiaxial system.
  • Older questions use NANDA and NIC. Your textbook uses ICNP. Check which one your professor expects.
  • If an answer defines prevalence as "new cases," it's wrong. New cases = incidence.
  • "Alzheimer's is the most prevalent disorder" only holds for people over 65 (10%) and over 85 (50%).

Psychiatric–mental health nursing

  • Defined as promoting mental health through assessment, diagnosis, and treatment of human responses to mental health problems and psychiatric disorders, across the lifespan and in every setting.
  • Guided by Psychiatric-Mental Health Nursing: Scope and Standards of Practice (ANA, APNA and ISPN, 2014). Its phenomena of concern include promoting health, preventing distress, co-occurring mental and substance or physical disorders, self-harm, violence, isolation, and low health literacy.

Trends and advocacy

  • Five trends: educational challenges (sicker patients, stays of about 3 days instead of about 2 weeks), a shortage of mental health professionals, an aging population, growing cultural diversity (majority-minority by 2044, so cultural competence matters), and technology (genetics, telehealth).
  • Advocacy is direct (reporting abuse or neglect, protecting confidentiality, supporting the patient's treatment decisions) or indirect (joining NAMI, following legislation, writing lawmakers when funding for vulnerable groups is cut).
Chapter 3

Neurobiology and Pharmacotherapy

How the brain sends messages, where psychiatric symptoms come from, and how each drug class changes the chemistry, including the side effects you must watch for.

What the brain regulates

  • Monitors the outside world and body fluids, controls skeletal muscles (including the diaphragm and speech muscles, so psych meds can depress breathing or slur speech), regulates internal organs, basic drives (hunger, thirst, sex, self-protection), memory, mood, thinking, sleep, and language.
  • Sleep: sleep disturbance shows up in virtually every psychiatric disorder, and better sleep is a marker of recovery. Give sedating meds at bedtime and stimulants early in the day.
  • Circadian rhythms: 24-hour cycles of sleep, body temperature, cortisol and neurotransmitters (NE, serotonin). They are altered in mood disorders, which is why you ask a depressed patient about their best and worst times of day.
  • Changes in basic drives (loss of sex drive, over- or undereating) can signal depression.

Neurons and neurotransmission

  • The brain has about 100 billion neurons. Each one responds to stimuli, conducts an electrical impulse (action potential: sodium in, potassium out), and releases neurotransmitters.
  • The neurotransmitter crosses the synapse and attaches to a receptor on the next cell, which either excites or inhibits it.
  • Afterward it is removed in one of two ways:
    • Destroyed at the receptor by an enzyme. Acetylcholine is broken down by acetylcholinesterase.
    • Reuptake: pulled back into the sending cell, then reused or destroyed by monoamine oxidase (MAO). This is how norepinephrine, dopamine and serotonin are cleared.
  • Too little signal can come from too little neurotransmitter or too few receptors; too much from excess release or oversensitive receptors.
Simply put

A neuron is a mail carrier. The neurotransmitter is the letter, the synapse is the street, and the receptor is the mailbox. After delivery, the letter gets shredded on the spot (acetylcholine) or carried back to the post office to be recycled or shredded by MAO (serotonin, NE, dopamine). Most psych drugs work by blocking the mailbox, blocking the return trip, or stopping the shredder.

Imaging the brain

Structural: what it looks like

  • CT: x-ray "slices"; shows atrophy, enlarged ventricles, infarcts. First choice to tell Alzheimer's from multiple strokes.
  • MRI: magnetic field; detailed structure

Functional: what it's doing

  • PET: radioactive glucose tracer shows activity and blood flow
  • SPECT
  • fMRI

Findings: ↓ frontal glucose use in schizophrenia, ↓ prefrontal activity in depression.

  • There is no good way to measure neurotransmitter levels directly. Much of what we know comes from how drugs work (e.g., D₂ blockers stop hallucinations, so hallucinations probably involve too much dopamine).
  • Concordance rate (twin studies) shows genetic risk: how often both identical twins have a disorder.

Simply put

Match the drug to the chemical problem. Schizophrenia = too much dopamine, so antipsychotics block dopamine. Depression = too little serotonin/NE, so antidepressants raise them. Anxiety = not enough GABA calming, so benzodiazepines boost GABA. Alzheimer's = too little ACh and too much glutamate, so we save ACh and block glutamate. ADHD meds raise dopamine and NE.

How drugs move and act

Pharmacokinetics

What the body does to the drug: ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion. Example: lithium is cleared by the kidneys, so renal function guides its dose and level checks.

Pharmacodynamics

What the drug does to the body: its receptor actions and effects.

Agonist vs. antagonist

  • Agonist: mimics the neurotransmitter and stimulates the receptor
  • Partial agonist: stimulates, but less
  • Antagonist: blocks the receptor

Pharmacogenetics

  • Poor metabolizers (slow CYP450 enzymes) get higher levels and more side effects; rapid metabolizers get less effect.
  • ~10% of Caucasians are poor CYP2D6 metabolizers (risperidone); ~20% of some Asian groups are poor CYP2C19 metabolizers (citalopram, diazepam).
  • Test patients of Asian ancestry for HLA-B*1502 before carbamazepine (Stevens-Johnson risk).

Simply put

Anticholinergic = "dries you up and slows you down": can't see, can't pee, can't spit, can't poop. Alpha-1 block = "blood pools in the legs when you stand," so you get dizzy. H₁ block = the Benadryl effect: sleepy and hungry.

Antianxiety and sleep medications

Benzodiazepines

  • How: bind the GABAA receptor and boost GABA's calming effect (more chloride enters, cell quiets down). No GABA present = no effect.
  • Examples: alprazolam (Xanax), clonazepam (Klonopin), diazepam (Valium), lorazepam (Ativan). Insomnia-only: flurazepam, temazepam, triazolam. Alcohol withdrawal seizures: chlordiazepoxide (Librium), diazepam, lorazepam. Drug of choice for status epilepticus.
  • Effects: antianxiety, hypnotic, anticonvulsant, amnestic, muscle relaxant. Fast onset, so best for acute anxiety.
  • Side effects: sedation (most common; tolerance develops), ataxia, anterograde amnesia, rare paradoxical agitation in children and older adults.
  • Alone they rarely stop breathing, but with alcohol or opioids they can cause fatal CNS depression.
  • Controlled substances; physical dependence develops. Withdrawal looks like the opposite of the drug: anxiety, insomnia, muscle tension, seizures. Taper, never stop abruptly.
Nursing priority
  • No driving or operating machinery until effects are known; no alcohol or other CNS depressants.
  • Older adults: falls, fractures, delirium; alternatives are usually preferred.
  • Patients with substance use disorders are usually given noncontrolled alternatives.

Antidepressants

  • Monoamine hypothesis: depression involves too little serotonin, NE, and/or dopamine. Receptors become "up-regulated"; raising the transmitters slowly "down-regulates" them, which may explain why antidepressants take 4–8 weeks.
  • Another theory: long-term use raises neurotrophic factors that help neurons survive and form new connections.
Simply put

Antidepressants work fast on chemistry but slow on mood. It's like repainting a room: the paint goes on day one, but it takes weeks for the whole room to dry and look different. Teach patients to keep taking it even if they feel nothing yet.

SSRI differences

Fluoxetine: most activating (insomnia, nervousness); very long half-life, once-weekly form exists.

Sertraline: most GI upset (diarrhea).

Fluvoxamine: most sedating; give at bedtime.

Paroxetine: most anticholinergic SSRI; caution in narrow-angle glaucoma.

Citalopram: dose-related QT prolongation; watch potassium and other QT drugs.

MAOI safety

Nursing priority
  • Tyramine (aged cheese, pickled or smoked fish, wine) can't be broken down, causing vasoconstriction and a hypertensive crisis.
  • No serotonergic drugs (SSRIs, buspirone, dextromethorphan) because of serotonin syndrome.
  • No sympathomimetics (stimulants, pseudoephedrine) because of hypertensive crisis.
  • Give a written list of foods and drugs to avoid.

Newer options

Esketamine (Spravato)

  • NMDA (glutamate) antagonist, nasal spray
  • Treatment-resistant depression (2 failed trials), with an oral antidepressant
  • Restricted program, given only under supervision; monitor at least 2 hours for dissociation and ↑ BP

Brexanolone (Zulresso)

  • First drug for postpartum depression; acts at GABAA
  • 60-hour IV infusion with continuous pulse oximetry
  • Excessive sedation risk: accompany the mother with her baby

Mood stabilizers

Lithium: the gold standard for bipolar disorder

  • A salt (cation like sodium and potassium). It likely stabilizes electrical activity, reduces glutamate, and enhances serotonin. It may reduce suicide risk.
  • Narrow therapeutic index: the toxic level is close to the effective level. Monitor blood levels regularly.
  • Levels go up with dehydration, declining kidney function, hydrochlorothiazide, ACE inhibitors (lisinopril), and NSAIDs (ibuprofen). Caffeine lowers levels.

Antipsychotics

First-generation (typical): phenothiazines, haloperidol and related drugs

They block D₂ receptors. That helps in one pathway and causes side effects in the other three.

Dopamine pathwayEffect of D₂ blockade
MesolimbicTherapeutic: reduces positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions)
Nigrostriatal (basal ganglia)EPS: acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism (days to months); tardive dyskinesia (months to years, may be permanent)
MesocorticalWorsens negative and cognitive symptoms
Tuberoinfundibular↑ Prolactin (dopamine normally holds prolactin down): gynecomastia, galactorrhea, amenorrhea, sexual dysfunction, bone loss
Simply put

Dopamine is the brakes on prolactin. Block dopamine, the brakes come off, prolactin rises, and you get breast changes and missed periods. Akathisia is the "can't sit still" feeling of inner restlessness.

Nursing priority
  • Screen for tardive dyskinesia with the AIMS (Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale).
  • Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS): rare but life-threatening. Muscle rigidity, altered mental status, high fever, unstable BP and HR. Treat immediately.
  • Other blockade: H₁ (sedation, weight gain), M₁ (anticholinergic effects that don't fade), α₁ (orthostatic hypotension).

Second-generation (atypical)

  • Block D₂ and 5-HT₂A. Blocking 5-HT₂A lets some dopamine through, so they cause less EPS and less prolactin rise. First-line; treat positive and negative symptoms. Several are also approved for depression.
  • Metabolic syndrome risk: weight gain, high glucose, high lipids. Weight gain comes from blocking H₁ and 5-HT₂C; M₃ block on the pancreas causes insulin resistance. Clozapine and olanzapine are highest risk.

ADHD and Alzheimer's medications

ADHD

  • Stimulants (first-line): methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta), amphetamines (Adderall, Vyvanse). Block NE and DA reuptake. Controlled. Insomnia, ↑ BP and HR, agitation, growth suppression; rarely psychosis or mania. Give early in the day.
  • Nonstimulants (less effective; for substance use, MAOI use, preference): atomoxetine (NE reuptake inhibitor; monitor BP, HR, liver); guanfacine and clonidine (α₂ agonists; titrate slowly; don't stop abruptly, rebound hypertension).

Alzheimer's disease

  • Cholinesterase inhibitors slow memory loss by saving ACh: donepezil (Aricept), galantamine (Razadyne), rivastigmine (Exelon). Side effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, incontinence, bradycardia and syncope. Less effective as disease progresses; abrupt stop can worsen cognition.
  • Memantine (Namenda): NMDA antagonist that blocks excess glutamate; moderate to severe disease; constipation, confusion. Often added to donepezil (Namzaric).
  • No current drug stops the degeneration.

Herbal supplements

Patients may think herbals are safer, cheaper, or worth trying when prescriptions didn't help. Many lack safety and efficacy data, they aren't FDA-regulated, and products may not contain what the label says. Ask about them in a neutral, nonjudgmental way.

Exam trap: Chapter 3
  • Gynecomastia comes from high prolactin, caused by dopamine blockade. Answers blaming "decreased prolactin" are wrong.
  • Clozapine: your textbook monitors the ANC, not the total WBC. The action is the same: low count means hold the dose and report immediately; a sore throat or fever is reported right away.
  • Lamotrigine does not treat acute mania. It's for maintenance and bipolar depression.
  • Paroxetine is the most anticholinergic SSRI, but still far less than a TCA like imipramine.
  • Phenothiazines block dopamine; a question may call it a "dopaminergic effect," but the movement side effects come from blockade.
  • FDA-approved anticonvulsant mood stabilizers are valproate, carbamazepine and lamotrigine. Gabapentin is not one of them.
  • The hypothalamus sits above the brainstem, not inside it.
  • SGA weight gain comes from blocking both H₁ and 5-HT₂C. H₁ is still the best single answer.
  • Tacrine (Cognex) is no longer sold in the U.S. If you see it as an option, it's a distractor.
Chapter 6

Legal Considerations

Admission and discharge, patients' rights, restraint and seclusion, confidentiality and its exceptions, torts, and documentation. Ethical principles are omitted.

Mental health law basics

  • Mental health laws are mostly state laws, so they vary. Learn your state's rules for clinical.
  • The Community Mental Health Centers Act of 1963 (Kennedy) drove deinstitutionalization: a shift from state hospitals to community care that respects civil rights.
  • Drug courts and mental health courts focus on treatment and diversion instead of punishment.
  • Parity laws (2008 Wellstone-Domenici, 2010 ACA) improve insurance access.

Every admission rests on four guidelines

  1. Admission status (voluntary or involuntary) does not decide whether the patient can make informed healthcare decisions.
  2. A well-defined psychiatric problem exists (DSM-5).
  3. The illness has created an immediate crisis and less restrictive options (outpatient care) are inadequate or unavailable.
  4. Hospitalization is reasonably expected to help.

  • Writ of habeas corpus: a patient who believes they're held without cause can petition the court to be freed. The hospital must submit it to the court immediately.
  • Least restrictive alternative doctrine: use the least drastic action that achieves the goal. If depression can be treated safely as an outpatient, hospitalization is too restrictive.
Simply put

Voluntary means "I checked myself in, and I can ask to leave." Involuntary means "a court decided I have to stay because I'm dangerous or can't take care of myself." Having a mental illness alone, stopping meds, or hearing voices is not enough; there has to be danger or grave disability.

Nursing priority
  • When a voluntary patient asks for discharge forms, get the forms and explore the decision ("Let's talk about why you want to leave"). Don't refuse, and don't just hand them over without assessing.

Patients' rights

Right to treatment

  • Free from excessive or unnecessary medication
  • Privacy and dignity
  • The least restrictive environment
  • Access to an attorney, clergy, and private care providers
  • No lobotomy, ECT, or similar treatments without fully informed consent

Right to refuse treatment

  • Patients can withhold, withdraw or retract consent at any time, verbally or in writing, even if involuntarily committed.
  • Emergency exception: if the person may cause serious, imminent harm to self or others, the facility can medicate without a court hearing.
  • Non-emergency forced medication requires a court hearing and all five criteria: serious mental illness; deteriorating function with suffering or threatening behavior; benefits outweigh harm; lacks capacity to decide; less restrictive options have failed.
  • Landmark cases: Rogers v. Okin (Massachusetts) and Rennie v. Klein (New Jersey) established that involuntarily hospitalized patients keep the right to refuse antipsychotics unless there's an emergency or a judicial finding of incompetence.
Nursing priority
  • If a patient says "Stop, I don't want that" as you prepare an injection, stop and ask about their concerns (often side effects). Giving it anyway is battery. A drawn-up syringe creates no obligation to give it.

Informed consent

  • Based on the right to self-determination (Canterbury v. Spence, 1972). The prescriber is legally responsible for obtaining it; the nurse educates and clarifies.
  • Required for surgery, ECT, and experimental drugs or procedures. Patients may refuse research and can voice grievances without reprisal.
  • The patient must be told: the condition; the nature and purpose of the treatment; its risks and benefits; alternatives; the probability of success; and the risks of not consenting.
  • Implied consent: the patient shows willingness, such as taking the pill you offer. The more risky or intrusive the procedure, the more you need explicit informed consent. If the patient doesn't understand, notify the prescriber.

Restraint and seclusion

  • Always the least restrictive means for the shortest time. In an emergency, less restrictive measures don't have to be tried first if staff judge they would be ineffective.
  • First rule out physical causes of agitation: drug interactions or side effects, fever, low blood sugar, hypoxia, electrolyte imbalance.
  • Try first: verbal intervention (ask for cooperation), reduce stimulation, active listening, diversion, PRN medication.

Nursing priority
  • In an emergency the nurse may restrain or seclude first and get an order as soon as possible after.
  • Orders are never PRN or standing. They can be renewed up to 24 hours total; then a physician or advanced practice provider must see the patient in person.
  • Release as soon as behavior is calmer and safer. Reapplying requires a new order.
  • Continuous observation; assess every 15–30 minutes for food, fluids, toileting, safety and comfort; document each check.
  • Document the specific behaviors, what was tried first and how it worked, the times placed in and released, and who ordered it. Protect the restrained patient from others (strangulation, assault).

Confidentiality

  • Confidentiality prohibits sharing privileged information without the patient's consent. Only the patient can waive it.
  • Never discuss patients in elevators or cafeterias, even without names. School paperwork never carries full identifiers.
  • HIPAA (1996) and its 2003 Privacy Rule: share only with those who need it to carry out the treatment plan. Psychotherapy notes get extra protection.
  • Releasing information to an employer, another facility or any third party requires the patient's signed consent stating what may be released.
  • Social media is never confidential and can be subpoenaed. "Disguised" posts have been traced back to patients.
  • Confidentiality continues after death.
  • Privileged communication (protection from testifying) covers APRNs in 12 states and psych RNs in 9. Elsewhere, nurses must answer a court's questions.
  • Sharing information with the treatment team is not a breach. Tell patients up front who will see what they say.

Simply put

What a patient tells you stays private, with one big exception: safety. If they threaten to hurt someone or a child is being abused, you have to tell. That's why you never promise to keep a secret: "I can't promise that. It might be important to share with the team."

Failure to protect

  • A suicidal patient left alone with the means to self-harm (an open sixth-floor window) is the nurse's liability.
  • Check identification before every medication; most psych patients are walking around.
  • Protect patients from other patients, from sexual misconduct, and from threatening family members; document domestic violence objectively.

Documentation

  • The record communicates between providers, supports continuity, quality improvement, risk management, utilization review and reimbursement, and serves as legal evidence (courts presume it true and untainted by memory).
  • Courts assume that care not documented did not happen.
  • Chart objective, specific observations and the patient's exact words ("Wore four layers of clothing. States, 'I need protection from evil bacteria'"), not vague labels ("appeared to hallucinate," "calmer").
  • Always document a patient's intent to harm self or others and what was done to protect them.
  • You're responsible for every entry under your password. Never share it, and never chart for someone else. If a colleague asks you to, refer it to the charge nurse.
  • Patients may see their records by facility protocol, but the record belongs to the institution.

Violence against nurses

Nurses' rate of workplace violence is about three times that of other jobs; 21% of nurses and students in one survey were physically assaulted in a year and over half verbally abused. Nurses have the same right as anyone not to be harmed. Newer laws increase penalties for striking healthcare workers. Prosecutors usually don't charge confused, delirious or psychotic patients, but violent patients, visitors and family who know what they're doing can be charged.

Exam trap: Chapter 6
  • Involuntary patients still have the right to refuse medication outside an emergency.
  • A verbal refusal counts. Giving the med anyway is battery, not negligence or malpractice (those require unintentional harm).
  • Threatening seclusion to keep a patient in their room is false imprisonment, even with the door open.
  • Mental illness, noncompliance, or hallucinations alone don't justify involuntary admission. Look for danger to self or others or grave disability.
  • Notifying a threatened victim is the duty to warn, not a confidentiality violation.
  • Seclusion for an adult can't be planned for 8 hours; orders are limited to 4 hours at a time.

Therapeutic Relationships

How a therapeutic relationship differs from a friendship, how to keep boundaries, Peplau's phases, and the nurse qualities that help patients grow.

Foundations

  • The nurse-patient relationship is the basis of all psychiatric nursing approaches.
  • Therapeutic use of self: using your own personality and talents to promote healing. A collaborative, respectful alliance is one of the best predictors of good outcomes.
  • Psychotherapy is theory-based talk therapy done by APRNs, psychiatrists and psychologists. It changes brain chemistry much like medication; the best treatment for most disorders combines both.
  • Counseling is what RNs do: a supportive face-to-face process to problem-solve, resolve conflicts, and feel supported.
  • Goals: help the patient express distressing thoughts and feelings, problem-solve for daily living, examine self-defeating thoughts and try alternatives, promote self-care and independence, educate about the disorder and medications, and promote recovery.
Personal (social) relationshipTherapeutic relationship
PurposeFriendship, socializing, enjoyment, getting a task doneThe patient's growth and goals

  • A small amount of self-disclosure is fine and can build connection (briefly answering "what's nursing school like?"), then refocus on the patient.
  • A therapeutic encounter is a short, informal interaction that can still help a lot when the nurse shows genuine concern, positive regard and empathy.
  • Clinical supervision is a mentoring relationship with feedback and evaluation; students get it from faculty and staff.

Boundaries

  • Boundaries are the "edge" of professional behavior and exist to protect patients. The nurse, never the patient, is responsible for keeping them.
  • There is a power differential even for students: you've read the chart, you're there to help, and you aren't the patient.

Signs of boundary crossing

  • Spending much more time with one patient than others
  • Doing tasks the patient can do alone
  • Sharing too much personal information
  • Thinking about the patient outside work
  • Getting defensive when others comment on the special attention ("I'm the only one who understands this patient")
  • Accepting personal comments and questions

Boundary violations

  • Accepting cash or expensive gifts
  • Planning a business together
  • Excessive touching
  • Trying to influence political or religious beliefs
  • Discussing a patient on social media
  • Social contact after discharge, such as inviting a patient to a staff picnic or to coffee

Transference and countertransference

Blurred roles are often caused by unrecognized transference or countertransference.

Simply put

Transference: the patient sees their mom when they look at you. Countertransference: you see your dad (or your grandmother, or your ex) when you look at the patient. A nurse who grew up with alcoholic parents and feels irritated by a patient with alcoholism is showing countertransference. Not every reaction is transference: if you break a promise, the patient's anger is realistic.

Values and beliefs

Our values reflect our own culture and choices and aren't right for everyone. Religion, politics, gender roles, drugs, sex and money can all cause conflict with patients. Self-awareness of your own values helps you accept differences. When a patient and nurse disagree on something like a right to die, the best analysis is that different values are being expressed, not that one of them is right.

Peplau's phases of the nurse-patient relationship

Hildegard Peplau (1952) described overlapping phases; a preorientation phase is added before them.

Preorientation

  • Prepare before meeting: chart, diagnosis, meds, labs, staff tips
  • Recognize your own fears and feelings
  • Learn unit safety rules (e.g., don't go into rooms alone)

Orientation

  • Introductions: your name, role, purpose; ask what the patient wants to be called
  • Build rapport and trust
  • Set the contract: place, time, date, duration
  • Explain confidentiality (who will know)
  • Bring up termination for the first time

Working

  • Gather more data
  • Identify problem-solving skills and self-esteem
  • Teach about the disorder and meds; symptom management
  • Patient explores problems and practices new coping
  • Evaluate progress

Termination

  • Summarize goals achieved
  • Review teaching; give handouts
  • Discuss using new coping skills and plans for the future
  • Review the relationship; share feelings and memories
  • Old feelings of loss or abandonment may resurface

Sounds like orientation

  • "I don't have any problems." (denial)
  • "It's so hard for me to talk about this."
  • "I don't see how talking to you will help."

Sounds like working

  • "I want to find a way to deal with my anger without becoming violent."
  • The patient names a problem and wants to change a behavior.
  • A contract shows the nurse works with the patient, not for them: both share responsibility.
  • Successful termination = summarizing and evaluating the changes together. Avoiding the topic, giving a phone number, or planning to meet later are all wrong.
  • When a new nurse takes over after a long relationship ends, they start again at orientation.
Simply put

Getting to know each other (orientation), doing the real work (working), saying goodbye well (termination). The goodbye is planned from the very first meeting so it never comes as a surprise.

What helps patients grow: genuineness, empathy, positive regard

Genuineness

Being open, honest and authentic; what's on the outside matches the inside (congruent communication). Use communication tools naturally, not like a parrot.

Empathy

Trying to understand the world from the patient's point of view and communicating that. An attitude, not a technique; possibly the most important quality.

Positive regard

Seeing the person as worthy of care, with strengths and potential. Shown through actions: attending (being fully present, such as staying with a crying patient), suspending value judgments, and helping patients develop their own resources.

Chapter 10

Stress Responses and Stress Management

How the body reacts to stress in the short and long term, what softens or worsens it, how to measure it, and which techniques lower it.

Stressors and early theories

  • Stressors: any physical or psychological stimulus that provokes a stress response. They can be acute or chronic, external or internal.
  • Physiological stressors: heat, cold, trauma, infection, hemorrhage, hunger, pain. Psychological stressors: divorce, job loss, debt, death, retirement, and even positive changes like marriage, a new baby, or unexpected success.
  • The body reacts the same way whether a stressor is real or perceived, negative or positive.
  • Walter Cannon: fight or flight. Sympathetic response: ↑ BP, HR, respirations and cardiac output. Criticized as too simple; some animals and people freeze instead.

Selye's general adaptation syndrome (GAS)

Hans Selye defined stress as "a nonspecific response of the body to any demand for change" and added the HPA axis to Cannon's model.

StageWhat happens
1. AlarmBrief, intense fight-or-flight. Eyes or ears send a signal to the amygdala; if it reads danger, it alerts the hypothalamus, which responds two ways:
Sympathetic: adrenals release epinephrine: faster HR, ↑ BP, rapid deep breathing, dilated pupils, sharper senses, glucose released, blood shunted from gut (dry mouth) and kidneys.
HPA axis ("the gas pedal"): CRH → ACTH → cortisol (↑ glucose and muscle endurance; digestion slows; immunity and memory affected).
If the threat passes, the parasympathetic system puts on the brakes (rest and digest).
2. Resistance (adaptation)Sustained, optimal resistance; stressors are usually overcome, with recovery and repair. Resources are being used up.
3. ExhaustionResistance fails, resources are depleted, stress becomes chronic. Long-term cortisol exposure brings anxiety disorders, major depression, sleep disorders, digestive problems, heart disease and weight gain.
Simply put

Alarm is slamming the gas when a car swerves at you. Resistance is driving hard for a long stretch; you can do it, but the tank is draining. Exhaustion is running on empty; the engine starts breaking down, and that's when stress-related illness shows up.

DistressEustress
KindNegative, drainingPositive, motivating ("eu" = good)
Leads toAnxiety, depression, confusion, helplessness, hopelessness, fatigueHappiness, hope, purposeful action
ExamplesDeath in the family, financial overload, job insecurity, losing a petA much-needed vacation, a favorite sport, a new baby, a new job challenge
  • Critique of GAS: different stressors may cause different responses, and degree matters. GAS best describes men; women often "tend and befriend" (protect the young, lean on social networks). Women are more vulnerable to stress disorders, possibly because they're more sensitive to CRH.

Stress and the immune system

  • Rats given saccharin with an immune-suppressing drug later suppressed their immunity from the saccharin alone: proof of a brain–immune connection (psychoneuroimmunology).
  • Short-term stress can prime immunity (cytokines released), but cytokines trigger corticosteroids that then inhibit the immune system. Prolonged stress suppresses immunity and lowers resistance to infection; wounds heal more slowly.
  • People with major depression have higher inflammatory cytokines; levels normalize with recovery. Interleukin chemotherapy can cause depression.

Mediators of the stress response

Mediators are what buffer or worsen the effect of a stressor for a particular person.

MediatorKey point
PerceptionThe perception of a stressor, not the stressor itself, drives the reaction. Shaped by age, gender, culture, life experience and lifestyle (a layoff hits a 40-year-old with a new baby harder than a financially secure 60-year-old).
Individual temperamentGenetics, childhood experiences, coping strategies and outlook form personality strengths and vulnerabilities.
Social supportStrong support buffers distress and is linked to lower mortality. Isolation raises illness and early death. An extremely important thing to assess.
Support groupsFor people facing similar events: AA (the prototype 12-step group), Gamblers Anonymous, Reach for Recovery, Parents Without Partners, online and video groups.
CultureMany Asian, African and Central American people express distress in somatic (bodily) terms and experience it physically.
Spirituality and religionEnhance immune function and well-being; prayer can elicit the relaxation response. Religious groups also provide belonging and social support. Support a patient who wants to use prayer.

Measuring stress and assessing coping

  • Holmes and Rahe Social Readjustment Scale (1967) weighted life events by severity to predict vulnerability to stress-related illness. Most events were rated even more stressful in 2007.
  • Recent Life Changes Questionnaire (revised version): add life change units (LCUs). High stress = 300+ in 6 months or 500+ in a year. Caveats: people and cultures perceive events differently, thresholds differ, and it equates change with stress.
  • Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10): measures how uncontrollable, unpredictable and overloaded life feels; no absolute cutoff.

Simply put

Stress points add up. Losing a job (68–79) and starting school (38) at the same time adds up to more than a single big event like a separation (76). When a question asks who needs the most thorough stress assessment, look for the person stacking changes.

Four coping styles to assess

  1. Health-sustaining habits: medical adherence, diet, relaxation, pacing energy
  2. Life satisfaction: work, family, hobbies, humor, spiritual comfort, arts, nature
  3. Social support
  4. Effective, healthy responses to stress
Nursing priority
  • First assess before you intervene: "Let's talk about what's going on in your life, then look at options." Ask how the patient currently copes.
  • Judge progress with objective data, such as blood pressure dropping from the 140s to the 120s, over subjective reports.
  • Anxious patient with high vital signs right now: start slow, deep breathing to activate the parasympathetic system.

Stress-management techniques

No single technique fits everyone; a mix works best. Relaxation helps anxiety, depression, insomnia and nightmares, and many medical conditions.

TechniqueHow it works
BiofeedbackInstruments show muscle activity, brain waves, skin temperature, HR or BP so a person can learn to control them. Smartwatches and trackers now do this too.
Deep breathingThe most common technique in the U.S. Slow abdominal breathing: in through the nose expanding the belly, hold 3 seconds, out slowly through the mouth; 2–5 minutes.
Guided imageryFocus on pleasant, calming images to replace stressful ones (a quiet mountain stream, floating deeper with each breath). Scripts should avoid unrealistic promises or things outside the patient's control. Reduces anxiety and pain.
Progressive muscle relaxationEdmund Jacobson, 1938. Tense a muscle group tightly for about 8 seconds, then release, moving feet to face or face to feet. Tension, not pain.
MeditationTrains the mind toward calm; creates a hypometabolic state that quiets the sympathetic system.
MindfulnessBeing in the present moment. STOP: Stop, Take a breath, Observe thoughts and feelings, Proceed.
Physical exerciseAt least 3 hours a week of any activity lowers depression risk; each extra 30 minutes lowers it about 17% more. Raises endorphins. Yoga adds meditation and calms the HPA axis.
Cognitive reframingFrom CBT. Recognize and replace irrational thoughts ("I'll never be happy until I'm as successful as my sister" becomes "I can find contentment at my own level"). Watch for overgeneralizations ("always," "never") and "should" statements.
JournalingIdentifies sources of stress and increases self-awareness; helps with worry and grief.
HumorMaking a stressful situation look absurd takes the sting out.

Everyday stress busters

  • Sleep 7–9 hours. Go to bed 30–60 minutes earlier; sleeping later disrupts body rhythms.
  • Exercise 150 minutes a week of moderate aerobic activity, at least 3 hours before bed.
  • Caffeine: no more than 4 cups a day; wean slowly.
  • Music, pets, massage.

Exam trap: Chapter 10
  • Your textbook never says exercise is "comparable to SSRIs." It says exercise lowers depression risk and raises endorphins.
  • In the alarm stage the adrenals release epinephrine. Heart rate and respirations go up under stress, never down.
  • The hypothalamus triggers the autonomic response; the sympathetic division is the one stimulated.
  • Sleeping in later is the wrong answer; going to bed earlier is right. Exercising right before bed disrupts sleep.
  • "Anorexia" in a stress question usually means loss of appetite, not the eating disorder.
