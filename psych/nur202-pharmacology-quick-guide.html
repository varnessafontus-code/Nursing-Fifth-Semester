Weeks 1–3 Consolidated Reference

Built from Chapters 3, 6, and 16 (Foundations of Psychiatric Mental Health Nursing)

This is a cross-reference, not a replacement for the chapter summaries. Use it to drill the concepts that show up again and again for the rest of the semester — every future clinical chapter assumes you already have this down.

1. The Synapse, in One Picture

A neuron fires an electrical signal down its length (sodium in, potassium out, charge flips negative→positive). At the end of the line, it releases a chemical messenger — the neurotransmitter — across the synapse (the gap) to receptors on the next neuron. That neurotransmitter either excites or inhibits the next cell. Then it’s cleared out one of two ways: - Enzymatic breakdown right at the site (e.g., acetylcholinesterase destroys acetylcholine). - Reuptake back into the original (presynaptic) cell, then broken down inside by monoamine oxidase (MAO) — this applies to norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin.

Nearly every psychotropic drug you’ll learn this semester works on one of these three points: how much neurotransmitter gets released, how long it stays in the gap before being cleared, or how well it binds the receptor once it’s there.

3. Core Concepts You’ll Be Tested On All Semester

Pharmacokinetics (PK) vs. Pharmacodynamics (PD): - PK = what the body does to the drug — absorption, distribution, metabolism (mostly liver), excretion. Think: the drug’s shipping and logistics. - PD = what the drug does to the body — receptor binding, mechanism, therapeutic effect, side effects. Think: what happens once the package is opened.

Agonist vs. Antagonist (lock-and-key analogy): - Agonist = a key that fits and turns the lock. Mimics the natural neurotransmitter, activates the receptor. - Partial agonist = turns the lock partway — some effect, but with a ceiling (e.g., aripiprazole at dopamine receptors). - Antagonist = a key that fits but doesn’t turn — and by sitting in the keyhole, physically blocks the real neurotransmitter from binding. Most antipsychotics are dopamine antagonists.

Reuptake inhibition (SSRIs, SNRIs, stimulants): - Picture a vacuum cleaner (the reuptake pump/transporter) sucking neurotransmitter back out of the synapse right after it’s released. A reuptake inhibitor jams the vacuum — neurotransmitter piles up in the gap and has more time/opportunity to bind receptors.

MAOIs: - MAO is the enzyme that destroys norepinephrine/dopamine/serotonin after they’re pulled back into the presynaptic cell. An MAO inhibitor stops that destruction — more neurotransmitter survives to be reused. (Same end result as an SSRI — more neurotransmitter available — but a completely different mechanism, which is why MAOIs carry different interactions, like the tyramine/hypertensive crisis risk with aged cheese and cured meats — worth knowing even though full detail comes later in the course.)

5. Deep-Dive: This Week’s Assigned Stimulants

Methylphenidate, amphetamine (Adderall), lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) — all CNS stimulants, first-line for ADHD.

  • Shared mechanism: block dopamine/norepinephrine reuptake; amphetamines additionally force extra release of these neurotransmitters into the synapse.
  • Why a “stimulant” calms hyperactivity: the effect targets underactive attention/impulse-control circuitry (prefrontal-limbic), not global arousal — more dopamine/NE availability there improves focus and reduces impulsivity/restlessness.
  • Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug — inactive until metabolized into active dextroamphetamine, giving a smoother onset/offset and somewhat lower misuse potential than immediate-release amphetamine. It’s also FDA-approved for moderate-to-severe binge-eating disorder in adults.
  • Shared side effects: insomnia (dose earlier in the day), appetite suppression, headache, jitteriness, ↑HR/BP. Growth suppression is a pediatric long-term concern — monitor height/weight.
  • Black box warning: high misuse/dependence potential — Schedule II controlled substances. Screen for personal/family substance use history; counsel on secure storage (diversion risk).
  • Watch for: rare but real risk of triggering/worsening psychosis or mania due to dopamine-potentiating effects — screen for personal/family history before starting.
  • Nonstimulant alternatives (for patients where stimulants are contraindicated or misuse risk is high): atomoxetine (norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, non-controlled, slow onset up to 6 weeks) and alpha-2 agonists clonidine/guanfacine (helpful for comorbid aggression/impulsivity; taper slowly to avoid rebound hypertension).

6. Deep-Dive: PTSD Medications (Chapter 16)

  • FDA-approved: sertraline (Zoloft), paroxetine (Paxil) — both SSRIs.
  • Off-label, commonly used: fluoxetine (Prozac), venlafaxine (Effexor, an SNRI).
  • Second-line: nefazodone, imipramine (TCA), phenelzine (MAOI).
  • Not recommended: atypical antipsychotics, citalopram, amitriptyline, topiramate, lamotrigine — weak evidence, unfavorable risk profile for this indication.
  • Big picture: medication is adjunctive. The primary, first-line treatment for PTSD is trauma-focused psychotherapy (TF-CBT, EMDR, exposure-based therapy) — meds manage comorbid depression/anxiety/sleep symptoms alongside it, they don’t replace it.

7. Legal Guardrails Around Medicating Psychiatric Patients (ties Ch. 6 to every drug above)

  • Patients retain the right to refuse psychotropic medication, even while involuntarily committed.
  • True emergency (imminent danger to self/others): can medicate without a prior court hearing.
  • Non-emergency override of refusal requires all five: serious mental illness present, deteriorating functioning or threatening behavior, benefits outweigh harms, patient lacks capacity for a reasoned decision, and less-restrictive options have already failed. Missing even one of these means you cannot legally override refusal outside a true emergency.
  • Informed consent (risks, benefits, alternatives, voluntary agreement) is required before starting most psychotropic medications — this is the prescriber’s legal responsibility, though nursing plays a major role in patient education and confirming understanding.
