Chapter 3: Neurobiology and Pharmacotherapy

Neuron communication — the analogy that makes this stick: think of a neuron as a relay runner passing a baton. The electrical signal (action potential) runs down the neuron’s length — sodium rushes in, potassium rushes out, and the charge flips from negative to positive and travels to the end of the cell. At the finish line (the synapse — the gap between neurons), the “baton” is a neurotransmitter, released from the presynaptic cell and caught by receptors on the postsynaptic cell. That catch either excites or inhibits the next runner. Every psychotropic drug in this course works by messing with some part of this baton pass: how much gets released, how long it stays in the gap, or how well the receiver catches it.

Neurotransmitters you must know cold (with disease associations):

Neurotransmitter Function ↓ Associated with ↑ Associated with
Dopamine (DA) Fine motor movement, emotion/thought integration, reward Parkinson’s disease, depression Schizophrenia, mania
Norepinephrine (NE) Mood, attention/arousal, fight-or-flight Depression Mania, anxiety, schizophrenia
Serotonin (5-HT) Mood, sleep, appetite, pain perception Depression —
GABA Main inhibitory (calming) transmitter; reduces anxiety/excitation Anxiety, schizophrenia, mania Reduced anxiety (this is the therapeutic direction — boosting GABA calms)
Glutamate Main excitatory transmitter; learning/memory Psychosis (NMDA underactivity) Neurotoxicity, Alzheimer’s neurodegeneration (chronic excess)
Acetylcholine (ACh) Learning/memory, parasympathetic (“rest and digest”) Alzheimer’s, Huntington’s, Parkinson’s Depression

Destruction of neurotransmitters — two mechanisms, both are drug targets: 1. Enzymatic breakdown at the postsynaptic cell — e.g., acetylcholinesterase destroys ACh. (This is exactly why cholinesterase inhibitor drugs for Alzheimer’s work — block the enzyme, and more ACh sticks around.) 2. Reuptake into the presynaptic cell, then broken down intracellularly by monoamine oxidase (MAO) — this is the mechanism for norepinephrine, dopamine, and serotonin. This is the single most important concept in the chapter, because it explains both SSRIs (block reuptake → more serotonin stays in the synapse) and MAOIs (block the enzyme that destroys the reused/leftover neurotransmitter → more available overall) with one underlying picture.

Agonist vs. antagonist — the lock-and-key analogy: - An agonist is a key that fits the lock and turns it — it mimics the natural neurotransmitter and activates the receptor. A partial agonist turns the lock partway — some effect, but capped (this is how aripiprazole behaves at dopamine receptors — not full-on, not fully blocked). - An antagonist is a key that fits the lock but doesn’t turn it — and because it’s sitting in the keyhole, it physically blocks the real key (the neurotransmitter) from getting in. This is how most antipsychotics work at dopamine receptors.

Major drug classes covered in this chapter (detailed cross-reference in the Pharmacology Quick Guide): - Antianxiety/hypnotics: benzodiazepines (enhance GABA by increasing chloride channel opening frequency → hyperpolarization → calming) and non-benzodiazepine options. - Antidepressants, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, psychostimulants, cholinesterase inhibitors — each targets a specific neurotransmitter system from the table above.

Pharmacokinetics vs. pharmacodynamics — don’t blur these: - Pharmacokinetics (PK) = what the body does to the drug (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion). Think of it as the drug’s logistics/shipping journey. - Pharmacodynamics (PD) = what the drug does to the body (receptor binding, mechanism of action, therapeutic and side effects). Think of it as what happens once the package is actually delivered and opened. - The liver metabolizes most drugs into active metabolites — chemicals that themselves have pharmacological action. This matters clinically for drug half-life, dosing intervals, and interactions.

Brain structures — high-yield locations: - Brainstem — most primitive; controls heart rate, breathing, sleep; connects to limbic system via mesolimbic/mesocortical pathways (dopamine reward pathway lives here — relevant to both psychosis and substance use). - Hypothalamus — basic drives (hunger, thirst, sex), links emotion to internal organ function, regulates sleep/wake cycle. - Cerebellum — coordinates voluntary movement, balance. - Limbic system — emotional regulation hub, uses NE/serotonin/dopamine. - Cerebral lobes: frontal (executive function, judgment, motor planning, insight — deficits here explain poor impulse control and impaired decision-making in many disorders), parietal (sensory integration), temporal (auditory, language comprehension, emotional expression via limbic connection), occipital (vision).

Meds note: this whole chapter is the meds section for the course going forward — every clinical chapter for the rest of the semester will lean on the neurotransmitter table and the agonist/antagonist framework above.

Answer Key & Rationales — Chapter 3 1. b — SSRIs block the serotonin transporter (reuptake pump), leaving more serotonin in the synaptic gap. 2. b — Benzodiazepines potentiate GABA-A receptor activity → increased chloride influx → hyperpolarization → reduced excitability. 3. c — Dopamine excess is the classic association with schizophrenia and mania. 4. b — Acetylcholine deficiency is central to Alzheimer’s pathology (hence cholinesterase inhibitor treatment). 5. b — PK = body’s handling of the drug; PD = drug’s effect on the body. 6. c — Full receptor activation mimicking the natural ligand = agonist. 7. b — Occupies the receptor without activating it, blocking the natural transmitter = antagonist. 8. c — Frontal lobe governs executive function, judgment, decision-making, impulse control. 9. b — MAO’s specific job is breaking down monoamines (NE, dopamine, serotonin) after reuptake. 10. b — Neurotransmitter levels shift fast, but downstream receptor sensitivity/intracellular changes take weeks — this is why antidepressant response is delayed even though the pharmacologic action starts immediately.

Chapter 10: Stress Responses and Stress Management

Two foundational models — know both, know how they differ: - Cannon’s fight-or-flight — sympathetic nervous system activation in response to a perceived threat: ↑ HR, ↑ BP, ↑ respirations, ↑ cardiac output. Critique: not everyone fights or flees — some freeze (think: deer in headlights), and the model doesn’t fully account for that. - Selye’s General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) — three stages: 1. Alarm — sympathetic activation, cortisol release (HPA axis engaged). 2. Resistance (adaptation) — body successfully manages the ongoing stressor; recovery/repair can happen if the stressor resolves. 3. Exhaustion — resources depleted, stress becomes chronic; linked to anxiety disorders, MDD, sleep disorders, cardiovascular disease, and weight gain. - Critique of GAS: it was modeled primarily on how males respond (fight-or-flight); research suggests females often show a “tend-and-befriend” pattern instead — protecting offspring and leaning on social networks rather than fighting/fleeing. Women also show greater sensitivity to corticotropin-releasing hormone, contributing to higher rates of some stress-related disorders.

Distress vs. eustress: distress = negative, draining stress (job loss, death in the family); eustress = positive, motivating stress (a new job, a wedding, a challenge). The physiological stress response looks similar for both — the body doesn’t fully distinguish “good” stress from “bad” stress.

Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACEs) — high-yield, dose-response relationship: abuse, neglect, household dysfunction (substance use, mental illness, incarceration, domestic violence in the home) experienced in childhood. More ACEs = more adult disease risk, in a graded (dose-response) fashion: - 4-to-12-fold increase: alcohol/substance use, MDD, suicide attempts. - 2-to-4-fold increase: smoking, poor self-rated health, STDs. - Graded increase: ischemic heart disease, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease. - This is the direct bridge into Chapter 16 — ACEs are the single strongest risk factor for later trauma-related and dissociative disorders.

Immune-stress connection: classic conditioning research (Ader & Cohen) demonstrated the brain can suppress immune function via learned association alone — proof that the nervous, endocrine, and immune systems are functionally linked, not separate silos. Chronic stress impairs wound healing and increases vulnerability to illness.

Coping styles/stress-reduction techniques (non-pharmacological, testable as nursing interventions): adequate sleep (7–9 hrs), aerobic exercise (~150 min/week), reduced caffeine, relaxation techniques (progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, deep breathing, mindfulness — the STOP technique: Stop, Take a breath, Observe, Proceed), journaling, humor, music, social support, and cognitive reframing.

Meds note: this chapter is deliberately non-pharmacological — it’s the counterbalance to Chapter 3. When a test question asks for a first-line nursing intervention for stress/mild anxiety, the correct answer is usually a relaxation/coping technique, not a medication. Save the benzodiazepine answer for actual anxiety disorders (Chapter 15, later in the course), not everyday stress.

Chapter 16: Trauma, Stressor-Related, and Dissociative Disorders

PTSD — four symptom clusters (DSM-5), must be able to sort symptoms into the correct cluster: 1. Re-experiencing — intrusive memories, flashbacks (dissociative reliving with vivid sensory detail), nightmares. 2. Avoidance — avoiding people, places, conversations, or activities that trigger trauma memories; accompanied by emotional numbing/detachment. 3. Negative alterations in cognition/mood — persistent negative beliefs, distorted blame (self or others), detachment, diminished interest in activities, inability to experience positive emotion. 4. Increased arousal/reactivity — hypervigilance, exaggerated startle response, irritability, difficulty sleeping/concentrating. - Onset can be delayed months to years after the trauma. Not everyone exposed to trauma develops PTSD — outcome depends on age, developmental stage, coping skills, support system, and preexisting neurobiology. - Epidemiology: ~60% of men and ~50% of women experience at least one lifetime trauma; ~8% of the population develops PTSD at some point. Men more often report combat/accident/disaster trauma; women more often report sexual abuse/assault. - In children: reduced/repetitive trauma-themed play, social withdrawal, self-blame, irritability, sleep disturbance. Rates of PTSD after specific traumas are striking: nearly 100% after witnessing a parent’s murder/sexual assault, ~90% after sexual abuse, ~77% after witnessing a school shooting.

Dissociative Identity Disorder (DID): - Two or more distinct personality states (“alters”), gaps in memory/identity, “losing time.” - ~90% have a history of childhood abuse/neglect — DID is fundamentally a trauma-response disorder, not a primary psychotic disorder (a classic distractor is confusing DID with schizophrenia — don’t). - Extremely high suicide risk: up to 70% of outpatients with DID have attempted suicide. Priority nursing intervention for a newly admitted patient = safety (1:1 observation), not memory recovery work. - Comorbid PTSD is nearly universal.

Nursing process for trauma-related disorders: - Priority nursing diagnosis: post-trauma response (or similar); also consider anxiety, impaired coping, social isolation, insomnia, chronic low self-esteem. - Implementation follows a stage model: (1) safety/stabilization first, (2) reduce arousal/regulate emotion (deep breathing, imagery, mindfulness), (3) improve socialization/reconnect supports, (4) help the patient build a coherent narrative of the event without taking on blame that isn’t theirs. - Health teaching: normalize the response (“this is a normal reaction to an abnormal event,” not a personal failure or weakness).

Meds note — this is the pharmacology payload of the chapter: - FDA-approved for PTSD: sertraline (Zoloft) and paroxetine (Paxil) — both SSRIs. - Off-label but commonly used: fluoxetine (Prozac), venlafaxine (Effexor — an SNRI). - Second-line if others fail: nefazodone, imipramine (TCA), phenelzine (MAOI). - NOT recommended due to weak evidence/adverse effect profile: atypical antipsychotics, citalopram, amitriptyline, topiramate, lamotrigine. - Medication is adjunctive — the primary, first-line treatment for PTSD is trauma-focused psychotherapy (TF-CBT, EMDR, exposure-based approaches), not medication alone. If a test question asks for the primary treatment modality, psychotherapy is the answer; medication treats comorbid depression/anxiety/sleep symptoms alongside it. - EMDR (eye movement desensitization and reprocessing): patient focuses on the traumatic memory while engaging in bilateral stimulation (eye movements, tapping, tones); helps process/integrate the memory. Requires additional certification beyond basic RN licensure.

Medication Review: Methylphenidate, Lisdexamfetamine, Amphetamine (Assigned This Week)

These three drugs are grouped together because they’re all CNS stimulants, primarily indicated for ADHD (some, like lisdexamfetamine, have additional FDA approval for binge-eating disorder). You’ll see them again in Chapter 11 (neurodevelopmental disorders) — this is a front-loaded review so the pharmacology sticks before you hit the clinical chapter.

Mechanism (shared across the class): these are sympathomimetic amines. Think of dopamine and norepinephrine as messages piling up at the mail slot (synapse) but getting picked up and taken away too fast to have much effect. Stimulants jam the pickup truck’s door shut — they block reuptake of dopamine and norepinephrine (methylphenidate) — and amphetamines go a step further, actually forcing extra release of these neurotransmitters into the synapse on top of blocking reuptake. More dopamine/NE sitting in the synapse for longer = better attention, less impulsivity, less restlessness in a brain whose prefrontal/limbic circuits are underactive. It’s paradoxical on its face — a “stimulant” calming down a hyperactive, impulsive brain — but the effect is targeted at underactive attention/inhibition circuits, not global arousal.

The three drugs, side by side:

Drug Class Key Notes
Methylphenidate (Ritalin, Concerta, Focalin) Reuptake inhibitor (blocks NE + dopamine reuptake) Widely used, extended-release options improve once-daily dosing and reduce insomnia
Amphetamine / mixed amphetamine salts (Adderall) Reuptake inhibitor + promotes additional NE/dopamine release Slightly broader mechanism than methylphenidate alone
Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse) Prodrug amphetamine (converted to active dextroamphetamine after ingestion) Once-daily; also FDA-approved for moderate-to-severe binge-eating disorder in adults; smoother onset/offset due to prodrug conversion, which is part of why it has somewhat lower misuse potential than immediate-release amphetamine

Side effects (all three, shared profile): insomnia (the big one — dose timing matters), decreased appetite/weight loss, headache, abdominal pain, jitteriness/anxiety, increased heart rate and blood pressure. Growth suppression is a concern with long-term pediatric use — height/weight need routine monitoring. Rarely, stimulants can trigger or worsen psychosis or mania (dopamine-potentiating effect) — assess for personal/family history of psychotic or bipolar disorders before starting.

Black box warning: high potential for misuse/dependence — these are Schedule II controlled substances. Assess for personal or family history of substance use disorder before prescribing; counsel patients to secure medication (diversion for nonmedical use, including sale, is a real risk on any campus or in any household).

Nursing considerations / patient teaching: - Dose earlier in the day (not later than early-to-mid afternoon) to minimize insomnia; extended-release formulations allow once-daily morning dosing with sustained coverage. - Monitor baseline and ongoing vital signs (HR, BP), height/weight (pediatric), and appetite. - Take with food if GI upset occurs; don’t crush extended-release formulations (defeats the release mechanism). - Educate that missing the “minimum effective dose” target and abrupt/inconsistent use, not the drug class itself, is what drives diversion/misuse risk — proper monitoring and secure storage matter. - Because lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug requiring metabolic conversion, its onset is somewhat slower/smoother than immediate-release amphetamine — useful to know if a question asks you to distinguish onset profiles.

Quick contrast — nonstimulant alternatives (useful for “why would you NOT choose a stimulant” questions): atomoxetine (a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, not a controlled substance, slower onset — up to 6 weeks for full effect, preferred if there’s comorbid anxiety, active substance use disorder, or tics) and the alpha-2 agonists clonidine/guanfacine (helpful for comorbid aggression/impulsivity; taper slowly — abrupt discontinuation risks rebound hypertension).

Bringing it together — the analogy version for your own recall: Picture the synapse as a mailroom. Methylphenidate jams the “return to sender” reuptake pump shut. Amphetamines do that and kick extra letters (neurotransmitter) out of storage into the mailroom. Either way, more dopamine/NE sits around longer, and a sluggish attention/impulse-control system gets the extra signal it needed. That’s also exactly why misuse potential exists — flooding that mailroom feels rewarding to a brain that doesn’t need the boost, which is the entire reason these are Schedule II.
