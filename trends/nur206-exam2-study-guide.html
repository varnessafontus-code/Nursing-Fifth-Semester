Delegation and supervision

Cherry Ch 20 · LaCharity Ch 1 · NCSBN/ANA 2019

Core definitions

  • Delegation (ANA): transferring responsibility for performing an activity to another person while keeping accountability for the outcome. The registered nurse (RN) can hand off the task. The RN cannot hand off the nursing process or nursing judgment.
  • Assignment: the distribution of work each staff member is responsible for during a shift, within their job description and scope. Assistive personnel (APs) are assigned to a registered nurse (RN), not to their own group of patients. Licensed practical nurses (LPNs) can be assigned specific patients, but the RN keeps assessment, care planning, and teaching.
  • Supervision: directing, guiding, and influencing how a task gets done. Whenever you delegate or assign, you supervise. It includes guidance, inspection, follow-up, and corrective action.

Who is accountable for what

Registered nurse (RN) (delegator)Delegatee (assistive personnel (AP), licensed practical nurse (LPN))
Deciding patient needs and when to delegate · being available · assessing and evaluating the outcome · stepping in to correct problems Their own actions · accepting only tasks within their competency · communicating back to the registered nurse (RN) · completing and documenting the task

The five rights of delegation

  1. Right task: allowed by policy and state law for that person.
  2. Right circumstances: the patient is stable and the task needs no independent nursing judgment.
  3. Right person: qualified and competent (verified, not assumed).
  4. Right direction and communication: clear instructions, expected results, and when and what to report back.
  5. Right supervision and evaluation: follow up, check the result, give feedback.

Who does what (LaCharity patterns)

Assistive personnel (AP)Licensed practical nurse (LPN)/licensed vocational nurse (LVN)Registered nurse (RN) only
Vital signs on stable patients · I&O · daily weights · activities of daily living (ADLs), hygiene, oral care · ambulating, positioning, turning · feeding (no swallow problem) · stool specimens · reminding patients of what the registered nurse (RN) already taught · reporting objective data (a blood pressure (BP) below a set number) By mouth (PO), subcutaneous (SubQ), intramuscular (IM) meds · dressing changes · wound cultures · data collection on stable patients (lung sounds, edema) · reinforcing teaching · tube feedings · stable, predictable patients Admission and initial assessment · care plans · initial and discharge teaching · evaluating response · unstable or new patients · intravenous (IV) push and blood transfusions · high-alert meds for unstable patients · interpreting labs and electrocardiograms (ECGs) · triage

Trap words. Assistive personnel (AP) can remind but cannot teach. AP can measure but cannot assess, monitor, or evaluate. A newly hired AP who was never trained on a glucometer cannot do fingersticks until the registered nurse (RN) verifies the skill.

Supervision levels (Cherry p. 866)

  • Unsupervised: registered nurse (RN) working with another RN as peers.
  • Initial direction and/or periodic inspection: the registered nurse (RN) knows this staff member's skills and has worked with them before.
  • Continuous: new working relationship, complex task, or a delegatee who is inexperienced or has not shown competence.

Assigning float and new nurses

  • Give a float nurse the patient that matches the skills from their home unit: a post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) float gets the post-colonoscopy patient under sedation; a surgical intensive care unit (SICU) float gets the fresh post-op renal stent patient; a med-surg float to coronary care unit (CCU) gets the heart failure (HF) patient on intravenous (IV) furosemide.
  • Give a new graduate or orienting registered nurse (RN) stable patients with predictable outcomes. Keep new admissions, fresh post-ops, unstable patients, and heavy teaching with experienced nurses.
  • A pregnant nurse avoids patients with airborne illnesses (varicella, shingles, tuberculosis (TB), measles). Contact-precaution patients (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE)) are fine.

Kid version

Delegation is like a coach sending a player into the game. The player runs the play, but the coach still owns the score. If the coach sends in a player who never learned the play, that is the coach's fault. The coach also has to watch the game, not leave the stadium.

Ch 15 · Information technology

Cherry pp. 651–678

Electronic health record (EHR)

A longitudinal electronic record of patient information from encounters in any setting. It also supports decision support, quality management, and outcomes reporting.

The Institute of Medicine (IOM)'s eight core electronic health record (EHR) functions:

  1. Health information and data capture
  2. Results management
  3. Order entry and order management (CPOE)
  4. Clinical decision support
  5. Electronic communication and connectivity
  6. Patient support
  7. Administrative processes
  8. Reporting and population health management

Key terms

  • Computerized provider order entry (CPOE) (computerized provider order entry): the provider types orders directly. It removes illegible handwriting and lost orders, generates related orders automatically (like a lab test for a drug level), flags duplicate or conflicting orders, and speeds up filling.
  • Clinical decision support: reminders (immunizations), drug dosing and interaction alerts, help interpreting data.
  • Meaningful use: a defined set of electronic health record (EHR) capabilities hospitals and providers must meet to qualify for Medicare incentive payments.
  • Interoperability: systems share data across settings in a standard, secure way.
  • Point-of-care technology: data retrieval and documentation at the bedside (computers on wheels (COWs), tablets, smartphones). Shifts nursing from relying on memory to using as-needed resources.
  • Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) (1996): privacy and security of protected health information. Every facility names a privacy officer. Compliance was required by April 2005. Violations carry civil and criminal penalties.
  • Biometrics: fingerprints, retina, iris, voice, or face used to grant access to data.

Judging health websites

Check the domain first: .edu education, .org nonprofit, .com commercial, .net internet provider, .gov government, .mil military. Then rate the site on authority (who wrote it), accuracy, objectivity (who pays for it), currency (date), and coverage.

Kid version

The electronic health record (EHR) is a shared notebook that every doctor and nurse can write in, and it is smart enough to tap you on the shoulder ("this drug fights with that drug"). Computerized provider order entry (CPOE) means the doctor types the order instead of scribbling it, so nobody has to guess at messy handwriting.

Cherry pp. 714–760

Leadership vs. management

Leadership is the ability to guide or influence others. Management is coordinating resources (time, people, supplies) to reach outcomes. People are led; things and activities are managed. A manager has an appointed position and formal authority. A leader may have neither.

Authority is the legitimate right to direct others, given by the organization. Power is the ability to get things done, with or without that right.

Five management functions (in order)

  1. Planning: deciding what to do (mission, goals, policies).
  2. Organizing: arranging structure and resources.
  3. Staffing: hiring, scheduling, orienting.
  4. Directing: leading, motivating, delegating, communicating.
  5. Controlling: measuring performance against standards and correcting (evaluations, budget review, quality improvement (QI)).

Manager roles: customer service provider, team builder, resource manager, change agent, clinical consultant, staff developer, mentor, corporate supporter.

Kid version

A leader is the kid everyone follows on the playground because they have a great idea for a game. A manager is the one holding the clipboard, making sure there are enough balls and that everyone gets a turn. The best charge nurse is both.

The five functions go Plan → Organize → Staff → Direct → Control. Like a birthday party: decide on the party, set up the room, invite helpers, run the games, then check what went well.

Four functions of a budget

Planning · communicating and coordinating · monitoring progress · evaluating performance.

Three types of budgets

Budget methods

  • Incremental: last period's budget plus or minus an adjustment. Most common, simple.
  • Zero-based: start from zero and justify every item each period.

Variance

Variance is the difference between budgeted and actual. Favorable = better than expected. Unfavorable = worse than expected. Quantitative analysis looks at the numbers. Qualitative analysis asks why (for example, patient acuity rose, so nursing hours per patient-day went from 5 to 7).

Formula frameworks (fill in the numbers yourself)

1 full-time equivalent (FTE) = 40 hours/week = 2,080 hours/year = 173.33 hours/month.

FTE = (hours worked per week) ÷ 40
Hours per patient-day (HPPD) = (total nursing hours worked) ÷ (number of patient-days)
Variance = (budgeted amount) − (actual amount)

Low productivity = many nursing hours with a low census (expensive). High productivity = few hours with a high census (may be unsafe).

The nurse's role

Prevent complications, readmissions, and hospital-acquired conditions (Medicare won't pay for "never events"). Use supplies wisely. Report frontline equipment needs for capital planning.

Kid version

The operating budget is your family's grocery and electric money every month. The labor budget is the part that pays everyone's paychecks. The capital budget is saving up for a car: it costs a lot and lasts for years.

Fixed costs are like rent, the same no matter how many people live in the house. Variable costs are like pizza, more people means more pizza.

Bloom's check

  1. Remember A new intravenous (IV) pump costs $1,800 and lasts 7 years. Which budget is it in, and what two conditions decide that? (p. 776)
  2. Apply A nurse works three 12-hour shifts a week. Set up the full-time equivalent (FTE) formula with her numbers and solve it.
  3. Analyze The unit spent more on overtime than planned because three ventilator patients were admitted. Is this variance favorable or unfavorable, and is the explanation quantitative or qualitative?
Answers
  1. Capital budget. It lasts more than 1 year AND costs more than the organization's minimum (often $500–$2,000). Both conditions must be met.
  2. Hours per week = 3 shifts × 12 hours = 36. Full-time equivalent (FTE) = 36 ÷ 40 = 0.9 FTE.
  3. Unfavorable (spent more than planned). The dollar difference is the quantitative part; the reason (three ventilator patients raised acuity) is the qualitative explanation.

Ch 19 · Communication and conflict

Cherry pp. 789–839

Positive communication

Building trust · "I" messages · eye contact · keeping promises · empathy · open-ended questions · clarifying · open body language · active listening.

Negative communication

  • Blocking: a noncommittal or general answer that shuts the patient down. ("Many people feel that way. It'll be okay.")
  • False assurance: promising an outcome you can't promise.
  • Conflicting messages: words say one thing, body language another.
  • Closed (yes/no) questions and closed body language (crossed arms, turning away).

Handoff report

Poor handoff communication is a leading contributor to sentinel events (The Joint Commission). Use a standard tool, allow time for questions, and include the patient and family when possible (bedside report).

Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation (SBAR), always in this order:

  1. Situation: who you are, the patient, what is happening now.
  2. Background: diagnosis, history, meds.
  3. Assessment: the data and your read of it.
  4. Recommendation: what you think needs to happen.

Closed-loop communication in a code: after giving the drug, say it out loud to the leader ("Epinephrine 1 mg intravenous (IV) given").

Social media

No patient names and no other identifiers: room number, facility, location, or age plus diagnosis. Deleted posts still exist and can be used in court. Board actions can include unprofessional conduct, unethical conduct, and revealing privileged communication. In the textbook case, a student posted a photo with the room number visible: the hospital was charged with a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) violation, the program lost the clinical site, and the student was expelled.

Rules: don't make it personal, use "I" messages, focus on the issue not the person, listen actively, treat the conflict as a shared problem. Conflict can be good: it brings out new ideas and shows where problems are.

Supportive environments (Gibb): empathy, equality, openness, positivity, supportiveness.

Ch 21 · Staffing and care delivery models

Cherry pp. 874–902

Staffing

Counting patients alone is a poor way to staff. Consider patient needs (number, acuity, setting, technology), nurse characteristics (education, competency, experience), and organizational needs (finances, licensing and accreditation such as The Joint Commission (TJC)).

Patient classification systems group patients by care needs to measure acuity. The higher the acuity, the more intense the care. American Nurses Association (ANA) factors include age and function, complexity, communication, cultural and language needs, severity, scheduled procedures, and psychosocial support.

Scheduling options: 8-, 10-, or 12-hour shifts, part-time weekend staff, job sharing, flextime, self-scheduling. Long consecutive shifts raise fatigue errors. Involving nurses in scheduling improves satisfaction and retention.

Renal

Hurst pp. 128–141 · LaCharity Ch 16

Glomerulonephritis (Hurst p. 128)

Inflammation in the glomerulus. Antibodies lodge there, scar it, and filtering drops. Main cause: strep. Acute can become chronic.

  • Signs: flank (CVA) tenderness, low urine output, hematuria, proteinuria, periorbital edema that spreads, high blood pressure (BP), fluid volume excess, high urine specific gravity, high blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine (azotemia), malaise and headache. Older adults may show fluid overload or confusion, sleepiness, and seizures that look neurologic.
  • Care: treat the strep, I&O, daily weights, diuretics, blood pressure (BP) checks, rest balanced with activity, dialysis if needed.
  • Fluid restriction: replacement = previous 24-hour losses + 500 mL. (Iggy: 500–600 mL)
  • Diet: more carbs, less sodium, less protein.
  • Teaching: diuresis starts 1–3 weeks after onset. Blood and protein can stay in the urine for months. Report signs of renal failure (malaise, headache, anorexia, N/V, less urine, weight gain).

Nephrotic syndrome (Hurst pp. 129–131)

Inflammation opens big holes in the glomerulus. Protein leaks into the urine → low albumin in the blood → fluid can't stay in the vessels and moves into tissue → the kidneys sense low volume → aldosterone holds sodium and water → that fluid also goes to tissue → anasarca (total body edema).

  • Signs: massive proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia, anasarca, hyperlipidemia. Loss of clotting-control proteins raises clot (thrombosis) risk.
  • Causes: infections, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), cancer, genetics, lupus, diabetes.
  • Treatment: diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors (block aldosterone), prednisone, cyclophosphamide (immunosuppresses, so infection is the major complication), lipid-lowering drugs, anticoagulation up to 6 months, dialysis.
  • Diet: moderate protein 1–2 g/kg/day (they get malnourished fast), low sodium. (Iggy: protein amount depends on glomerular filtration rate (GFR))
  • Nursing: daily weights, I&O, abdominal girth or limb size, skin care.

Hurst rule: limit protein in kidney problems except nephrotic syndrome.

Iggy adds · glomerulonephritis (GN) and nephrotic syndrome

  • Glomerulonephritis (GN) antibiotics: penicillin, erythromycin, or azithromycin. Finish all of them.
  • Older adults with glomerulonephritis (GN) can look like they have heart failure (HF) (dyspnea, jugular venous distention (JVD), crackles) or a neuro problem (confusion, seizures).
  • Nephrotic: albumin under 3 g/dL; cholesterol and triglycerides rise because the liver overcompensates; fat in the urine; renal vein thrombosis is a clot risk.
  • Nephrotic skin is edematous and breaks down easily: meticulous skin care, infection precautions.

Phases, in order: initiation (injury) → oliguric (output can fall to 100 mL/24 hr or less; ends in about 10–14 days) (Iggy: oliguria = under 400 mL/day; 100 or less = anuria) → diuretic (output rises; replace fluid and electrolytes based on labs) → recovery (3–12 months; increase protein and calories, resume activity).

Signs: blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine up · specific gravity up, then fixed (kidney can't concentrate or dilute) · hypertension (HTN) and heart failure (HF) from fluid retention · anorexia, N/V from toxins · uremic frost (skin care) · phosphorus up, calcium down (pulled from bone) · anemia (not enough erythropoietin) · hyperkalemia (lethal dysrhythmias) · metabolic acidosis (can't excrete H+ or keep bicarb).

Goals: prevent complications, manage symptoms, remove the cause. Assess: hourly output, central venous pressure (CVP), urinary tract infection (UTI) signs, blood pressure (BP), nephrotoxic meds.

  • Nursing: bed rest (lowers metabolism), turn-cough-deep breathe, I&O, daily weights (1 kg = 1,000 mL), close vitals.
  • Meds: loop or osmotic diuretics · high potassium (K) → intravenous (IV) glucose + insulin, IV calcium gluconate for dysrhythmias, sodium polystyrene sulfonate · phosphate binders (prevent low calcium (Ca)) · IV meds in the smallest volume allowed.
  • Diet: more carbs and fats (calories), low protein, avoid phosphate, avoid high-K foods (bananas, citrus, coffee).
  • Infection prevention: aseptic technique, skin and mouth care, prevent pressure injuries, avoid catheters, keep away from infectious people.

Contrast dye: hold metformin and resume 48 hours after the procedure only if kidney function and creatinine are normal (Hurst p. 72).

Iggy adds · acute kidney injury (AKI)

  • Diuretic-phase polyuria can be the first sign of recovery. Watch for dehydration, low potassium (K), and low sodium (Na).
  • Hyperkalemia, in order: intravenous (IV) calcium gluconate (protects the heart; does not lower potassium (K)) → IV regular insulin + glucose (shifts K into cells) → a K binder like polystyrene sulfonate (removes K) → loop diuretic or dialysis. Continuous electrocardiogram (ECG). No K supplements or K-sparing diuretics.
  • Fluid allowance = urine output + 500 mL.
  • Protein: about 0.6 g/kg/day if not on dialysis; 1–1.5 g/kg/day on dialysis. Avoid phosphate (dairy, colas) and potassium.
  • Contrast risk is highest with creatinine ≥1.5 or glomerular filtration rate (GFR) under 60, diabetes, heart failure (HF), dehydration, older age, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), metformin, or contrast in the past 72 hours. Hydrate before and after; acetylcysteine may be ordered; goal output about 150 mL/hr for 6 hours afterward.

Renal replacement therapy (Hurst pp. 135–139)

Start when: blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine can't be lowered · fluid overload is compromising heart and lungs · hyperkalemia or metabolic acidosis won't respond to treatment.

Hemodialysis (the machine acts as the glomerulus; 3–4 times a week):

  • Watch what they eat and drink between treatments. Assess fluid status before starting. Watch electrolytes and blood pressure (BP) throughout.
  • Heparin during hemodialysis (HD) → bleeding precautions, avoid invasive procedures.
  • Watch for depression (suicide risk). Unstable cardiovascular patients can't tolerate hemodialysis (HD).
  • Access: arteriovenous (AV) fistula (artery joined to vein) or AV graft (synthetic). Both need surgery and weeks to mature. Blood flows 300–800 mL/min. Arterial end pulls blood out; venous end returns it. Temporary catheters go in the internal jugular or femoral vein.
  • Access arm: no blood pressure (BP), no needle sticks, no intravenous lines (IVs) or blood draws, no constriction. Feel the thrill, hear the bruit to confirm it's patent.

Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT): intensive care unit (ICU), continuous, never more than 80 mL of blood outside the body, so no big fluid shifts. For fragile cardiovascular status with acute kidney injury (AKI).

Peritoneal dialysis: the peritoneum is the filter. 2,000–2,500 mL of warmed dialysate runs in by gravity (about 10 minutes) through a Tenckhoff catheter, dwells, then drains with the toxins. One cycle = an exchange. Warm it so vessels dilate.

  • Outflow should be clear, straw-colored (you can read newsprint through it). Cloudy = infection.
  • Not draining? Turn the patient side to side.
  • Continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD): 4 exchanges a day, 7 days a week; needs a motivated patient who can learn. Not for disc disease or arthritis (back pressure) or a colostomy (infection).
  • Automated peritoneal dialysis (APD): cycler runs overnight while asleep.
  • Complications: exit-site infection; peritonitis is the major one (abdominal pain, cloudy effluent).
  • Diet: more fiber (fluid slows peristalsis) and more protein (lost with each exchange).

Kidney stones (Hurst p. 140)

  • Pain with N/V, white blood cells (WBCs) in urine, hematuria. Suspect a stone → get a urine specimen for red blood cells (RBCs) right away, and give pain medication right away.
  • Treatment: ondansetron, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or opioids, alpha blockers (relax the ureter), increase fluids, lithotripsy (ESWL) or surgery, strain urine and send the stone.
  • After lithotripsy, the best sign it worked: sand-like sediment in the urine bag.
  • Iggy: tamsulosin (alpha blocker) relaxes the ureter so the stone passes.
  • Workbook triage: a patient with blood pressure (BP) 90/40 is seen before a stone patient with 8/10 pain (perfusion beats pain).

24-hour urine: discard the first void and start the clock, save every void after, collect the final void at the 24-hour mark. Missed one = start over. Keep stool and toilet paper out.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) and dialysis extras (LaCharity Ch 16)

  • Fluid restriction about 500–700 mL plus the previous day's output. Phosphate binders (sevelamer) with meals.
  • Epoetin alfa subcutaneous (SubQ): check blood pressure (BP) first; hold for severe hypertension (HTN). Risk rises when Hgb goes above 11.
  • After hemodialysis (HD), decreased level of consciousness (LOC) may be dialysis disequilibrium syndrome → call the provider.
  • Transplant acute rejection: fever, hypertension (HTN), graft pain, falling output → increase immunosuppressants.
  • Gentamicin: watch blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and creatinine.

Kid version

Kidneys are coffee filters. Prerenal: no water poured in. Intrarenal: the filter is torn. Postrenal: the cup underneath is blocked. In acute kidney injury (AKI) the filter clogs first (almost no pee), then the floodgates open (tons of watery pee), then it slowly heals.

In nephrotic syndrome the filter has big holes, so protein (albumin) falls through. Albumin is the sponge that keeps water inside your blood vessels. Without the sponge, water leaks into your whole body and you swell everywhere.

Peritoneal dialysis uses your belly lining as the filter. Pour warm fluid in, let it soak up the trash, then drain it out. Clear means clean. Cloudy means germs.

Answers
  1. 1,200 + 500 = 1,700 mL for the next 24 hours (fluid replacement = previous 24-hour losses + 500 mL).
  2. Nephrotic syndrome is losing protein through big holes in the glomerulus, so albumin is low and the patient becomes malnourished fast. Moderate protein (1–2 g/kg/day) replaces what's lost. Hurst's rule: limit protein in kidney problems except nephrotic syndrome.
  3. First patient: peritonitis (cloudy effluent + abdominal pain), the major peritoneal dialysis (PD) complication; report it. Second patient: turn her side to side to help the fluid drain.
  4. Sand-like sediment in the bag. That's the crushed stone passing. No pain or a draining catheter doesn't prove the stone was broken up.
  5. Calcium gluconate → insulin + glucose → potassium (K) binder. Calcium doesn't lower K at all; it stabilizes the heart muscle so the peaked T waves don't become a lethal rhythm while the other two work. Insulin moves K into cells fast but temporarily. The binder actually removes K, but slowly.

Terms and cardiac output (Hurst pp. 79–81)

  • Preload: blood returning to the right side and the stretch it causes (stretch releases atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)).
  • Afterload: the pressure (resistance) in the aorta and arteries that the left ventricle pumps against. Hypertension (HTN) raises afterload, lowers forward flow, and wears the heart out → heart failure (HF).
  • Cardiac output = heart rate (HR) × stroke volume. Affected by heart rate and rhythm, blood volume (less volume = less cardiac output (CO)), and contractility (myocardial infarction (MI), meds, cardiomyopathy).

Low cardiac output shows up everywhere: level of consciousness (LOC) down · chest pain · wet lungs and short of breath · cool, clammy skin · urine output down · weak pulses. Arrhythmias don't matter until they drop cardiac output (CO). Three always do: pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT), ventricular fibrillation (VF), asystole (no output → cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) now).

Chronic stable angina (Hurst pp. 81–84)

Temporary ischemia from low oxygen (O₂) during exertion. Relieved by rest and/or nitroglycerin.

  • Nitroglycerin sublingual (SL): dilates veins and arteries (lowers preload and afterload) and coronary arteries. 1 every 5 minutes, up to 3. Dissolve, don't swallow. Dark glass bottle, cool and dry. It may burn or fizz. Expect a headache and a blood pressure (BP) drop. Replace tablets about every 6 months, spray every 2 years. (Iggy: tablets every 3–5 months)
  • Nitroglycerin (NTG) algorithm: take 1 sublingual (SL); if pain is the same or worse after 5 minutes, activate emergency response.
  • Beta blockers (propranolol, metoprolol, atenolol, carvedilol): lower blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), and contractility → less cardiac work. Check BP and pulse first; they can drop cardiac output (CO) too far.
  • Calcium channel blockers (nifedipine, verapamil, amlodipine, diltiazem): dilate arteries and coronaries → lower afterload, more oxygen (O₂) to the heart.
  • Aspirin 81–325 mg per provider.
  • Teaching: rest often, don't overeat (low fat, high fiber), limit caffeine, wait 2 hours after eating to exercise, dress warmly in cold, take nitroglycerin (NTG) before exertion, stop smoking, lose weight, avoid isometric exercise, reduce stress. Testing rule: do everything you can to decrease the heart's workload.

Iggy adds · angina drugs

  • Nitroglycerin (NTG) at home: take 1 → wait 5 minutes → not better or worse → call 911, then keep taking 1 every 5 minutes (3 total) while waiting.
  • Nitroglycerin (NTG) in the hospital: recheck pain and vitals 5 minutes after each dose. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) under 100, or 25 mm Hg below the last reading → lower the head of bed (HOB) and notify the provider.
  • No sildenafil, tadalafil, or vardenafil within 24–48 hours of nitrates: profound, possibly fatal hypotension.
  • Nitroglycerin (NTG) patch: clean, hairless skin; rotate sites; take it off 12–14 hours a day (prevents tolerance); remove before defibrillation.
  • Beta blockers: hold and call if heart rate (HR) is under 50–60 or systolic blood pressure (SBP) under 100. Avoid in asthma (bronchospasm); they can mask hypoglycemia.
  • New chest pain at home: chew aspirin 325 mg and call 911.
  • High-intensity statin (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin) for every acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patient before discharge, whatever the lipid results.

Cardiac catheterization (Hurst pp. 84–85)

  • Before: ask about iodine or shellfish allergy (Iggy: shellfish isn't cross-reactive; ask about any prior reaction to contrast dye), check kidney function (dye leaves through the kidneys; acetylcysteine may be ordered). A warm "hot shot" and palpitations during dye injection are normal.
  • After: vital signs (VS), watch the site for bleeding and hematoma, check the limb for the 5 Ps (pulselessness, pallor, pain, paresthesia, paralysis), bed rest flat with the leg straight 4–6 hours (Iggy: leg straight 2–6 hours, head of bed (HOB) up to 30° if allowed), report pain at the site at once. Hold metformin 48 hours.
  • Iggy timing: clear liquids up to 2 hours before, solids 6 hours. After: vitals every 15 min × 1 hr, every 30 min × 2 hr, then every 4 hr. Encourage fluids to flush the dye.
  • Major complication: hemorrhage. Earliest sign: rising heart rate (HR) (LaCharity).

Acute coronary syndrome (Hurst pp. 85–90)

Unstable angina = impending myocardial infarction (MI). Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) pain comes without exertion and is not relieved by rest or nitroglycerin (NTG).

  • Crushing pressure radiating to neck, jaw, arms, or shoulder blades; dizziness, sweating, N/V; cold and clammy; blood pressure (BP) drops.
  • Women: gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, indigestion or fullness, unusual fatigue, can't catch their breath, jaw ache. Older adults: shortness of breath. Women, older adults, and diabetics often deny symptoms and delay care.
  • ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is the one to worry about: cath lab for percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 90 minutes.
  • In the emergency department (ED): oxygen if oxygen saturation (SpO₂) is under 90%, aspirin (chewable), nitroglycerin, morphine. Head of bed up to lower workload. (LaCharity: hold nitro and morphine if hypotensive.)
  • Fibrinolytics (alteplase, tenecteplase, reteplase, streptokinase): door-to-drug 30 minutes, give within 12 hours of pain onset. Major complication: bleeding. Absolute contraindications: intracranial tumor or bleed, suspected aortic dissection, internal bleeding. Take a bleeding history; draw labs when starting intravenous lines (IVs) to limit punctures; no arterial blood gases (ABGs); bleeding precautions (watch gums, urine, stools; electric razor, soft toothbrush, no intramuscular (IM) injections).
  • Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) (angioplasty, stents): major complication is myocardial infarction (MI) from reocclusion, plus site bleeding. Chest pain after PCI → call the provider at once.
  • Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG): for multivessel disease or left main occlusion ("widow maker": sudden death risk).
  • Cardiac rehab: stop smoking, increase activity gradually, low fat, salt, and cholesterol, no isometrics, no Valsalva or straining (docusate, no suppositories). Sex after about 1 week to 10 days if no complications, in the morning when rested. Walking is the best exercise.

Iggy adds · acute coronary syndrome (ACS), fibrinolytics, coronary artery bypass graft (CABG)

  • 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) within 10 minutes of a chest-pain report. Repeat troponin in 3–6 hours.
  • More absolute contraindications to fibrinolytics: ischemic stroke within 3 months, significant head or face trauma within 3 months.
  • Signs a fibrinolytic worked: pain stops abruptly, reperfusion dysrhythmias, ST segments return toward baseline, markers peak early.
  • After coronary artery bypass graft (CABG): report chest-tube drainage over 150 mL/hr. Heavy drainage that suddenly stops → think clot and tamponade. Atelectasis is the #1 complication (splint, cough, deep breathe, walk). Atrial fibrillation (A-fib) often shows up on days 2–3.

Heart failure (Hurst pp. 91–96)

Causes: coronary artery disease (CAD), cardiomyopathy, valve disease, endocarditis, myocardial infarction (MI), hypertension. Systolic heart failure (HF) = can't contract and eject. Diastolic heart failure (HF) = can't relax and fill.

Left-sided ("Left = Lungs")Right-sided (backs up into veins)
Pulmonary congestion, dyspnea, cough, blood-tinged frothy sputum, restlessness, tachycardia, third heart sound (S3), orthopnea, nocturnal dyspnea Distended neck veins, edema, enlarged organs, weight gain, ascites
  • Diagnosis: B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) (sensitive; can be positive before the chest x-ray shows anything; turn nesiritide off 2 hours before drawing it), chest x-ray (CXR) (big heart, infiltrates), echo (ejection fraction, valves), New York Heart Association (NYHA) class 1–4 (4 is worst).
  • Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors are the drug of choice: block angiotensin I → II, dilate arteries. Angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) block angiotensin II receptors. Both block aldosterone → lose sodium (Na) and water, keep potassium (K).
  • Beta blockers are first-line alongside angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors. Core measure: discharge on an ACE inhibitor and/or beta blocker to lower afterload and keep blood moving forward.
  • Digoxin (0.5–2.0 ng/mL): stronger contraction, slower rate → cardiac output (CO) and kidney perfusion go up. Check the apical pulse first. Toxicity: early anorexia, N/V; late arrhythmias and vision changes. Low potassium (K) + digoxin = toxicity; any electrolyte imbalance raises the risk.
  • Diuretics (furosemide, hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ), bumetanide, triamterene-HCTZ, spironolactone) lower preload. Give in the morning. Diuresis is good in heart failure (HF).
  • Severe or decompensated heart failure (HF): intravenous (IV) inotropes (milrinone, dobutamine), vasodilators (nitroprusside, nesiritide, nitroglycerin).
  • Low-sodium diet lowers preload. Salt substitutes can be loaded with potassium; canned foods and over-the-counter (OTC) meds hide sodium. Head of bed (HOB) up. Weigh daily; report a gain of 2–3 lb (1–2 kg) in a day (Iggy: report 1–2 lb overnight or 3 lb in a week). Testing rule: fluid retention → think heart first.

Iggy adds · heart failure (HF) drugs

  • Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors: first-dose hypotension, dry cough, high potassium (K), kidney function. Never combine with an angiotensin II receptor blocker (ARB). Angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs): high K, but no cough.
  • Beta blockers: start low, go slow; heart failure (HF) can look worse at first, so weigh daily. Resting heart rate (HR) goal about 55–60.

Pacemakers (Hurst pp. 96–97)

  • For symptomatic bradycardia. Demand pacers fire only when needed; fixed pacers fire constantly. The rate can go above the set rate, but worry if it drops below.
  • After placement: watch the incision. Most common complication: electrode displacement. Immobilize the arm, no lifting it above the shoulder, passive range of motion (ROM) to prevent frozen shoulder.
  • Malfunction: loss of capture (stimulus with no contraction), failure to sense (fires at the wrong time). Causes: bad programming, dislodged electrode, depleted battery. Watch for low cardiac output (CO) or a falling rate.
  • Teaching: check pulse daily, carry an identification (ID) card, avoid strong electromagnetic fields and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) care is the same.

Iggy adds · pacemakers

  • No raising the arm above the shoulder and no lifting more than 10 lb for about 4 weeks.
  • Check the pulse daily for a full minute; report a rate below the set rate.
  • Cell phone on the opposite side; household appliances are fine; avoid strong magnets and transmitters; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is usually contraindicated.
  • Don't press on or twist the generator; avoid tight clothing over it. Wear a medical alert bracelet.

Pulmonary edema (Hurst pp. 98–99)

  • At risk: fast intravenous (IV) fluids, very young or very old, heart or kidney disease. Often happens at night after lying down.
  • Sudden breathlessness, restless and anxious, severe hypoxia, pink frothy sputum. Iggy: older adults may show only new confusion.
  • Priority: high-flow oxygen, keep oxygen saturation (SpO₂) above 90%. Sit upright with legs down (pools blood in the legs).
  • Furosemide 40 mg intravenous (IV) push over 1–2 minutes (too fast → hypotension, ototoxicity). Bumetanide 1–2 mg IV over 1–2 minutes. IV nitroglycerin (lowers afterload). Morphine 2 mg IV (dilates vessels). Nesiritide infusion no longer than 48 hours.
  • Prevent it: check lung sounds often, avoid fluid overload.

Cardiac tamponade (Hurst p. 100)

Blood or fluid fills the pericardial sac and squeezes the heart. Causes: chest trauma (car crash), right ventricle (RV) biopsy, myocardial infarction (MI), pericarditis, bleeding after coronary artery bypass graft (CABG).

  • Hallmarks: central venous pressure (CVP) up, blood pressure (BP) down. Muffled heart sounds, distended neck veins, shock, equal pressures in all 4 chambers, narrowed pulse pressure. (Widened pulse pressure → think increased intracranial pressure (ICP).)
  • Treatment: pericardiocentesis, surgery.

Iggy adds · tamponade

  • Beck triad: jugular venous distention (JVD) (with clear lungs), muffled heart sounds, hypotension.
  • Pulsus paradoxus over 10 mm Hg: systolic blood pressure (BP) drops more than 10 during inspiration.
  • After coronary artery bypass graft (CABG): heavy mediastinal chest-tube drainage that suddenly stops = clotted tube, blood collecting around the heart.
  • Notify the provider at once; intravenous (IV) fluids support output while the diagnosis is confirmed. After pericardiocentesis, watch for it to come back.

Kid version

Preload is how much water fills a balloon before you squeeze it. Afterload is how hard it is to push the water out through a tiny straw. High blood pressure makes the straw tinier, so the heart works harder and gets tired.

"Left = Lungs." When the left side is weak, blood backs up into the lungs like a traffic jam, so the person sounds wet. When the right side is weak, the jam backs up into the body: puffy legs and big neck veins.

Tamponade is like the heart wrapped in a water balloon that keeps filling. The heart can't open up to fill, so blood pressure falls while the neck veins bulge.

Pancreatitis: the pancreas digesting itself (Hurst pp. 142–144)

The pancreas makes insulin (endocrine) and digestive enzymes (exocrine). #1 cause: gallbladder disease. #2: alcohol.

  • Signs: pain that gets worse with eating · distention and ascites (protein-rich fluid leaking into the belly) · swollen pancreas mass · rigid, board-like abdomen with guarding (bleeding → peritonitis) · Cullen sign (bruising around the navel) and Grey Turner sign (flank bruising) · fever · N/V · jaundice · hypotension from bleeding or ascites.
  • Labs: lipase (most specific) and amylase up · white blood cell count (WBC) up · glucose up · alanine aminotransferase (ALT)/aspartate aminotransferase (AST) up · prothrombin time (PT)/activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) longer · bilirubin up · H&H down if bleeding, up if dehydrated.
  • Goal: control pain. Keep the stomach empty and dry: nothing by mouth (NPO), nasogastric (NG) tube to suction, bed rest. (Iggy: NPO + intravenous (IV) fluids for everyone; NG to suction only for severe vomiting or ileus)
  • Meds: patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) opioids (morphine, hydromorphone), fentanyl patch · anticholinergics to dry the stomach (benztropine, diphenoxylate/atropine) · gastrointestinal (GI) protectants (pantoprazole, famotidine, cimetidine, antacids).
  • Fluid and electrolyte balance · ease back into a diet · insulin (the pancreas is sick, and total parenteral nutrition (TPN) adds glucose) · daily weights · no alcohol; refer to Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) if alcohol is the cause.
  • LaCharity: watch for hypocalcemia (Chvostek, Trousseau) and shock. In Case Study 9 the pancreatitis patient has the highest acuity.

Iggy adds · pancreatitis

  • Life-threatening complications: hemorrhagic pancreatitis, hypovolemic shock, acute kidney injury (AKI), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multiorgan failure.
  • Calcium and magnesium may need intravenous (IV) replacement.
  • Chronic pancreatitis: pancreatic enzymes with every meal; avoid alcohol, nicotine, high-fat foods, and large meals.

Cirrhosis (Hurst pp. 144–146)

The liver detoxifies the body, helps blood clot, breaks down drugs, and makes albumin. In cirrhosis, scar tissue replaces liver cells and blocks blood flow through the liver → portal hypertension.

  • Signs: firm, nodular liver · jaundice · abdominal pain (stretched capsule) · chronic indigestion · bowel changes · ascites · enlarged spleen · fatigue · peripheral edema · anemia · can progress to hepatic encephalopathy and coma.
  • Testing strategies: sick liver → #1 concern is bleeding · lower medication doses · never give acetaminophen (antidote: acetylcysteine) · avoid narcotics (giving one is like double dosing) · enlarged spleen = immune system involved.
  • Diagnosis: low albumin, high alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), ultrasound, computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), liver biopsy (confirms).
  • Liver biopsy: check prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) and vitals first. Position supine with the right arm behind the head; patient exhales and holds the breath (moves the diaphragm out of the way). After: lie on the right side (pressure on the site); watch vitals for hemorrhage.
  • Treatment: antacids, vitamins, diuretics · no alcohol · I&O and daily weights (ascites = fluid problem) · rest · bleeding precautions · measure abdominal girth · skin care for jaundice · diet: less protein, low sodium. (Iggy: moderate protein, no strict restriction)
  • Paracentesis (removing ascites fluid): have the patient void first, position sitting up, check vitals. Pulling off fluid can cause shock: blood pressure (BP) drops, pulse rises.

Hepatic encephalopathy / coma (Hurst pp. 146–147)

Normally: protein → ammonia → the liver turns it into urea → kidneys excrete urea. A failing liver can't convert it, so ammonia builds up and level of consciousness (LOC) drops.

  • Signs: mild mental and motor changes · hard to wake · asterixis (hand-flapping tremor) · handwriting changes · decreased reflexes · slow electroencephalogram (EEG) · fetor (breath smells like ammonia). Anything that raises ammonia (like protein) makes it worse. Liver patients tend to be gastrointestinal (GI) bleeders.
  • Treatment: lactulose (lowers ammonia), enemas, monitor serum ammonia.

Iggy adds · cirrhosis and encephalopathy

  • Most common cause of cirrhosis in the U.S.: hepatitis C. Alcohol and fatty liver disease are also big causes.
  • Paracentesis: voiding first is the most important pre-step (prevents bladder puncture). Weigh before and after; measure and describe the fluid.
  • Ammonia triggers: gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding (blood is a protein load), infection, hypovolemia, low potassium (K), constipation, sedatives and opioids.
  • The ammonia level doesn't always match the symptoms: treat the patient, not the number.
  • Lactulose goal: 2–3 soft stools a day and less confusion. Too many stools → low potassium (K) and dehydration; protect perineal skin.
  • Rifaximin or neomycin kill the gut bacteria that make ammonia (neomycin is nephrotoxic, not long-term).
  • Safety first for the confused patient: falls, aspiration. Check level of consciousness (LOC) and asterixis often.

Bleeding esophageal varices (Hurst pp. 147–148)

Portal hypertension forces new veins to form in the stomach, esophagus, and rectum. They cause no trouble until they rupture. A patient with alcohol use disorder who is gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding usually has varices.

  • Replace blood · monitor vitals and central venous pressure (CVP) · oxygen (anemic patients need it) · octreotide (lowers liver pressure) · beta blockers (lower portal pressure, prevent rebleeding).
  • Esophageal variceal ligation (banding) is first-line; endoscopic sclerotherapy injects a sclerosing agent.
  • Balloon tamponade (Sengstaken-Blakemore tube): emergency only, holds pressure on bleeding varices, no more than 12 hours.
  • Enemas to clear blood from the gut, lactulose (blood is protein → ammonia), saline lavage to clear the stomach.
  • Iggy: two large-bore intravenous lines (IVs) for an upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleed; transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) for recurrent bleeding or ascites.

Peptic ulcer disease (Hurst pp. 148–150)

Erosion in the esophagus, stomach, or duodenum; a common cause of gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding. Burning mid-epigastric or back pain, heartburn.

  • Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD): nothing by mouth (NPO) before, sedated, NPO until the gag reflex returns. Watch for perforation: pain, bleeding, trouble swallowing.
  • Upper gastrointestinal (GI) series: nothing by mouth (NPO) after midnight; no smoking, gum, mints, or nicotine patches (they speed motility and raise secretions → aspiration).

Iggy adds · peptic ulcer disease

  • H. pylori triple therapy: proton pump inhibitor (PPI) + 2 antibiotics for 10–14 days. Finish every dose.
  • Gastric ulcers: usually ages 50–70, acid normal or low, slightly higher cancer risk. Duodenal: ages 20–50, too much acid, no added cancer risk.
  • Hemorrhage is the most common complication: coffee-ground or bright red vomit, melena, blood pressure (BP) down, heart rate (HR) up, H&H down, dizziness.
  • Perforation: sudden sharp pain spreading across the abdomen, rigid board-like belly, patient curls into the fetal position → surgical emergency.
  • Pyloric obstruction: vomiting, metabolic alkalosis, low potassium (K).

Inflammatory bowel disease (Hurst pp. 152–154)

  • Ulcerative colitis: large intestine only. Crohn's (regional enteritis): mostly the ileum, but anywhere in the small or large intestine.
  • Signs: diarrhea, rectal bleeding, vomiting, weight loss, cramping, dehydration, blood in stools, anemia, fever, rebound tenderness (push in, let go, it hurts = peritoneal inflammation).
  • Colonoscopy (most common test): clear liquids 12–24 hours before, nothing by mouth (NPO) 6–8 hours, no nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), laxatives or enemas until clear, polyethylene glycol prep (serve it ice cold), sedated. After: assume the worst and watch for perforation (pain or unusual discomfort). Barium enema if the colonoscopy is incomplete.
  • Diet: low residue (slows motility, saves fluid); avoid cold foods and smoking (both speed motility).
  • Meds: antibiotics, steroids, biologics (infliximab, adalimumab), aminosalicylates (sulfasalazine, mesalamine).
  • Surgery: ulcerative colitis (UC) → total proctocolectomy with an ileostomy, or a Kock pouch (nipple valve, no external bag) or ileal pouch–anal anastomosis (IPAA) (ileum attached to rectum; most popular). Crohn's → avoid surgery; remove only the affected section.

Ostomy care (Hurst p. 154)

  • Ileostomy: drains liquid constantly; never irrigated. Avoid hard-to-digest, rough foods. Electrolyte drinks in summer. Always slightly dehydrated → kidney stone risk.
  • Colostomy: the farther along the colon, the more water is absorbed and the more formed the stool. Ascending/transverse → semi-liquid. Descending/sigmoid → semi-formed or formed.
  • Irrigate descending and sigmoid colostomies for regularity: same time every day, after a meal, left side like an enema. Cramping → stop the flow, lower the bag, check the fluid temperature.
  • Hurst practice item: a colostomy has no swimming restrictions; previous activities can resume.

Appendicitis (Hurst p. 155)

  • #1 worry: rupture.
  • Pain starts generalized, then settles in the right lower quadrant (RLQ) (McBurney point); rebound tenderness; anorexia. History: pain first, then N/V.
  • White blood cell count (WBC) up; ultrasound, computed tomography (CT). No enemas, laxatives, or heat (rupture). Sudden pain relief followed by worse, spreading pain = rupture.
  • Usually laparoscopic surgery. After abdominal surgery: Fowler's position; never put pressure on a suture line.

Total nutrient admixture (TNA) / total parenteral nutrition (TPN) (Hurst p. 156)

  • Refrigerate; let it sit out a few minutes before hanging. Central line, filter, dedicated line (nothing else through it), on a pump.
  • Hang no longer than 24 hours; new tubing with each bag; may cover with a dark bag. Mixed daily to match electrolytes, so don't mix ahead.
  • Glucose checks every 6 hours, urine for glucose and ketones, may need insulin, daily weights.
  • Taper off gradually to avoid hypoglycemia. (If a bag runs out before the next arrives, hang 10% dextrose in water (D10W).)
  • Most frequent complication: infection. Home total nutrient admixture (TNA): hand washing.
  • Iggy rescue: refeeding syndrome (low phosphorus, low potassium (K), heart failure (HF), edema, seizures, breathing problems) → call the provider. Fat emulsion overload (fever, high triglycerides, clotting problems) → stop the infusion.
  • Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube: after insertion, bed rest 6 hours; watch vitals, temperature, level of consciousness (LOC), bleeding, abdominal pain. Clean and check the site daily.

Central line insertion (Hurst p. 157)

  • Have saline flush ready. Position Trendelenburg (distends neck veins). No fluids until the chest x-ray confirms placement and rules out pneumothorax.
  • Air in the line → left side, Trendelenburg (traps air in the heart's apex).
  • Changing tubing: clamp it, or have the patient do the Valsalva (deep breath and hum).

Kid version

Pancreatitis is the pancreas eating itself with its own digestive juices. Every time you eat, the pancreas squirts more juice, so it hurts more. That's why we keep the stomach empty and dry: no food, nasogastric (NG) tube sucking out what's there.

The liver is a cleaning factory. In cirrhosis the factory is scarred. It can't clean ammonia, so the brain gets foggy. Lactulose is a trash bag that grabs ammonia in the gut and carries it out with the poop. The jammed factory also backs up traffic (portal hypertension), so new side roads (varices) form. They're thin and can burst.

Hiatal hernia: the stomach is trying to climb up, so stay sitting up. Dumping: food is racing out of the stomach, so lie down on your left side to slow it down.

Hyperthyroid diagnosis and treatment (Hurst pp. 55–57)

  • Thyroid scan: stop iodine-containing meds 1 week before; wait 6 weeks to restart. Ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or computed tomography (CT) may be used. (Amiodarone is high in iodine and can affect the thyroid.)
  • Eye care for exophthalmos: sunglasses, cool compresses, lubricating drops, head of bed (HOB) up.
  • Antithyroid drugs: methimazole (once daily, fewer side effects) and propylthiouracil (PTU) stop hormone production. Used pre-op. Goal: euthyroid.
  • Iodine (saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI), Lugol's): shrinks the gland and its blood supply. Give in milk or juice through a straw (stains teeth).
  • Propranolol: supportive; lowers heart rate (HR), blood pressure (BP), and anxiety. Testing strategy: avoid beta blockers in asthma or diabetes.
  • Radioactive iodine: one by mouth (PO) dose. Rule out pregnancy first. Destroys thyroid cells → patient becomes hypothyroid. Radiation precautions: stay away from babies and don't kiss anyone for 1 week.
  • Thyroid storm = hyperthyroidism × 100; can rebound after radioactive iodine.

Iggy adds · hyperthyroidism and thyroid storm

  • Methimazole: avoid crowds and sick people (low immunity); causes birth defects. Weight gain, slow heart rate (HR), or cold intolerance = dose too high. Propylthiouracil (PTU): liver toxicity (dark urine, jaundice).
  • Give iodine (saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI), Lugol's) 1 hour after the thionamide.
  • Never palpate the thyroid of a hyperthyroid patient: it can dump hormone and trigger storm.
  • Radioactive iodine: relief takes 6–8 weeks; sit to urinate and flush 2–3 times; don't share utensils or toothbrushes.
  • Storm signs: fever (even 1°F above baseline → report), tachycardia and dysrhythmias, agitation → psychosis, seizures, coma; abdominal pain, N/V/D.
  • Storm actions: airway first → antithyroid drug, then iodide → propranolol with continuous cardiac monitoring → cooling blanket plus acetaminophen (no aspirin: it frees more thyroid hormone) → glucocorticoids and normal saline (NS).

Thyroidectomy (Hurst p. 57)

For a large goiter, cancer, or failed drug therapy.

  • Post-op priority: hemorrhage. Report pressure in the neck; check for bleeding at the incision and behind the neck.
  • Recurrent laryngeal nerve damage: listen for hoarseness. Both cords paralyzed → airway obstruction → emergency trach.
  • Trach set at bedside for swelling, nerve damage, or hypocalcemia.
  • Hypocalcemia from parathyroid removal (tingling, Chvostek, Trousseau: the "not sedated" picture).
  • Teach how to support the neck, keep items close, head of bed (HOB) up to reduce edema, more calories pre- and post-op.
  • Hurst practice item: shortness of breath (SOB) and neck pressure after thyroidectomy → elevate the head of bed (HOB), remove the dressing to look for bleeding, and stay with the patient.

Hypothyroid treatment (Hurst p. 58)

Levothyroxine or liothyronine on an empty stomach, for life. Energy goes up as it works. These patients often have coronary artery disease (CAD), so watch for myocardial infarction (MI) when starting. Hurst practice item: a patient on levothyroxine reports chest fullness → don't give it; call the provider. (Iggy: a chest-pain protocol may also get a 12-lead within 10 minutes)

Diet, exercise, and drugs (Hurst pp. 70–74)

  • Diet: count carbs; high fiber (slows absorption, smooths spikes). Plate method on a 9-inch plate: ½ non-starchy vegetables, ¼ carbs, ¼ protein, plus water or a low-calorie drink.
  • Exercise: regular, same time and amount daily, when blood sugar is at its highest; wait until glucose is normal; eat something first to prevent hypoglycemia.
  • Metformin: first choice for type 2; lowers liver glucose production and helps glucose into cells; doesn't cause hypoglycemia. Stop for contrast dye or surgery; resume 48 hours later if creatinine is normal. (Iggy: hold 24 hours before; lactic acidosis risk with kidney impairment)
  • Non-insulin injectables (dulaglutide, semaglutide, pramlintide): daily, every 2 days, or weekly dosing; some weight loss.
  • Insulin: required for all type 1. Starting dose by body weight, about 0.4–1 unit/kg/day, adjusted until glucose is normal and urine has no glucose or ketones.
  • Mixing: regular is clear, neutral protamine Hagedorn (NPH) is cloudy → draw up clear first. Long-acting insulins are clear; never mix them or give intravenous (IV). Regular is the standard IV insulin.
  • Basal-bolus: long-acting once daily + rapid-acting divided before meals. No snacks required, but food must be available with rapid-acting. Pre-meal goal 80–130. Eat when insulin peaks (that's when sugar is lowest).
  • Pumps use only rapid-acting insulin (basal + meal boluses). Rotate sites within one area first. Illness raises blood sugar (testing strategy: illness = diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)).

Hypoglycemia (Hurst p. 75): ≤70 mg/dL

Cool, clammy, shaky, confused, headache, nervous, nausea, fast pulse, hungry. Give 15 g simple carb (skim milk, apple juice, raisins; not chocolate or cookies, because fat slows absorption) → recheck in 15 minutes → repeat if needed → then complex carb or protein.

Unconscious diabetic patient: treat as hypoglycemic. 50% dextrose in water (D50W) intravenous (IV) (large-bore IV) or glucagon subcutaneous (SubQ), intramuscular (IM), or IV if there's no IV access. Prevention: eat, take insulin regularly, know the signs, check glucose.

Iggy: give 30 g if glucose is under 50; in the hospital, skip orange juice (potassium). After glucagon, turn the patient on the side (it can cause vomiting); repeat in 10 minutes if still unconscious.

Iggy adds · diabetes

  • Numbers: diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) glucose over 300, pH under 7.35, bicarbonate (HCO₃) under 15, ketones positive. Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS) glucose over 600, osmolality over 320, no ketones.
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) potassium: starts normal or high, then crashes once insulin works. Replace once potassium (K) is under 5.0 and urine output is at least 30 mL/hr. Bicarbonate only if pH ≤7.0.
  • Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) resolved: glucose under 200, bicarbonate (HCO₃) over 18, pH over 7.30, anion gap under 12. Start subcutaneous (SubQ) insulin once the patient can drink and ketosis has stopped.
  • Insulin times: rapid (lispro, aspart) onset 10–30 min, give within 10 min of a meal · regular onset 30–60 min · neutral protamine Hagedorn (NPH) peak 6–14 hr (cloudy; bedtime snack) · glargine has no peak.
  • Nothing by mouth (NPO) patient: still give basal insulin (often reduced); hold rapid-acting and premixed.
  • Sick days: never skip insulin; glucose every 2–4 hr; check urine ketones; 8–12 oz sugar-free fluid every hour awake. Call if glucose stays over 250, ketones are moderate or large, fluids won't stay down, or illness lasts over 1–2 days.
  • Type 1 vigorous exercise only when glucose is 100–250 with no urine ketones.
  • Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists (semaglutide, dulaglutide) and dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors: report severe abdominal pain radiating to the back (pancreatitis). Pramlintide never goes in the same syringe as insulin.

Long-term complications (Hurst p. 78)

  • Vessel damage everywhere: retinopathy (blindness), nephropathy (kidney damage).
  • Neuropathy: impotence; foot pain, paresthesia, numbness; neurogenic bladder (incontinence or retention); gastroparesis (aspiration risk).
  • Foot care: cut nails straight across, dry well between toes, well-fitting shoes, inspect feet daily, no harsh chemicals.
  • Higher infection risk.

Kid version

The thyroid is the body's gas pedal. Hyper = pedal stuck down: hot, fast, skinny, jittery. Hypo = foot off the gas: cold, slow, tired, gaining weight.

Insulin is a key that unlocks the cell door so sugar can go in. Potassium sneaks in through the same door. That's why giving lots of insulin in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) drops potassium fast.

Addison = not enough steroid: everything runs low (blood pressure (BP), sugar, sodium) except potassium. Cushing = too much steroid: a puffy "cushion" body that bruises, gets infections, and runs high on sugar and BP.

Hyperparathyroid = too much calcium, so the body feels "sedated," sleepy and weak. Hypoparathyroid = not enough calcium, so muscles twitch and cramp.

Answers
  1. Safety: rule out pregnancy before the dose, and for 1 week stay away from babies and don't kiss anyone (radiation). Long-term: it destroys thyroid cells, so expect to become hypothyroid and need lifelong thyroid replacement. Also watch for thyroid storm as a rebound.
  2. Give fludrocortisone, I&O, daily weight. Not low potassium (K) monitoring (Addison causes high K) and not a low-sodium diet (they're losing sodium and need more).
  3. Treat as hypoglycemic. 50% dextrose in water (D50W) through a large-bore intravenous (IV), or glucagon subcutaneous (SubQ)/intramuscular (IM)/IV if there's no IV access. Low sugar kills brain cells fast; a little extra sugar in a hyperglycemic patient does little harm.
  4. 1. Start normal saline (NS) → 2. Start intravenous (IV) insulin → 3. Hourly glucose and potassium (K) → 4. Switch to 5% dextrose in water (D5W) when glucose is about 250–300 (and expect K to be added).
  5. Take it on an empty stomach with water, 30–60 minutes before breakfast, and keep calcium (and iron, antacids, fiber) about 4 hours away. Food and calcium both block absorption.
  6. Aspirin frees more thyroid hormone from its carrier proteins, which feeds the storm. Acetaminophen plus a cooling blanket lowers the fever without that effect.
