Fluids & IV solutions

Your F&E What & Why guide Sections 1–3 · Hurst workbook pp. 1–7

The big rule

For every imbalance ask: (1) Is this a water problem or a particle problem? (2) What does this ion do to nerves and muscles? Answer both and you can predict the symptoms instead of memorizing lists.

Where the water is and how it moves

  • Total body water ≈ 60% of adult weight (less in older adults and people with more fat; more in infants). ICF ≈ 2/3 (K⁺ is the main cation); ECF ≈ 1/3 (Na⁺ is the main cation), split into plasma and interstitial fluid.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (uses ATP) keeps Na⁺ out and K⁺ in. Break cells open (burns, crush, TLS) and K⁺ floods out → hyperkalemia.
  • Osmosis: water moves toward the saltier side (water follows sodium). Hydrostatic pressure (BP) pushes fluid out of capillaries; oncotic pressure (albumin) pulls it back. Serum osmolality 275–295 mOsm/kg.
  • Edema = push beats pull: ↑ hydrostatic (HF, overload), ↓ oncotic (low albumin: liver disease, malnutrition, burns), or leaky capillaries (burns, sepsis).
Like I'm 10

A soaker hose with tiny holes. Blood pressure pushes water out the holes; albumin is a sponge inside the hose soaking it back in. Turn the pressure up, take the sponge away, or poke bigger holes, and the garden floods.

Red flag

New crackles, pink frothy sputum, or sudden severe dyspnea = pulmonary edema. Sit up, O₂, notify the provider.

Third spacing: fluid trapped where it can't circulate (tissue, ascites, pleura) from burns, sepsis, low albumin, pancreatitis, bowel obstruction, major surgery. The client can be swollen and hypovolemic at the same time; when it shifts back (burns after ~48–72 h), watch for overload. Like money in a locked savings account: it's on paper, but your card still declines.

Pattern: low Ca and low Mg look alike (twitchy). High Ca and high Mg look alike (sluggish, ↓ reflexes). Both high and low K cause weakness; the ECG and history tell them apart.

Sodium = water balance + brain

Hyponatremia (< 135)

  • Too much water: SIADH, excess hypotonic fluid or water, HF, cirrhosis. Sodium loss: vomiting, diarrhea, NG suction, thiazides, adrenal insufficiency, sweating replaced with plain water
  • Water moves into brain cells → cerebral edema → headache, confusion, seizures, coma
  • Dilutional → fluid restriction first. Sodium loss → NS; 3% NaCl if severe/symptomatic
  • Correct slowly (often ≤ ~8 mEq/L in 24 h) or risk osmotic demyelination. Seizure precautions

Hypernatremia (> 145)

  • Water loss or no access to water: DI, fever, sweating, watery diarrhea, confused/intubated/older adults, tube feedings, excess hypertonic saline or bicarb
  • Water pulled out of brain cells → thirst, dry sticky mucosa, flushed skin, restlessness → seizures
  • Replace water gradually (0.45% NaCl, D5W, oral water); too fast → cerebral edema

Potassium = muscles and the heart

About 98% of K⁺ lives inside cells, so small serum changes matter (4 → 6 is a 50% jump). The kidneys excrete ~90% of it.

Hypokalemia (< 3.5)

  • Loop/thiazide diuretics, vomiting, NG suction, diarrhea, laxatives, insulin, alkalosis, steroids, poor intake, low Mg
  • Weakness, leg cramps, ↓ reflexes, shallow breathing, constipation/ileus
  • ECG: flat T, U wave, ST depression. Raises digoxin toxicity risk
  • Oral K with food and a full glass of water; K-rich foods; check Mg (K won't stay corrected until Mg is)

Hyperkalemia (> 5.0)

  • Kidney failure (most common), spironolactone, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, acidosis, cell destruction (burns, crush, TLS), transfusion, adrenal insufficiency
  • Hemolyzed sample reads falsely high: redraw if it doesn't fit
  • Twitching, paresthesias → weakness → flaccid paralysis; cramping, diarrhea
  • ECG: tall peaked T → prolonged PR, flat P → wide QRS → V-fib/asystole
IV potassium safety
  • Never IV push, IM, or subcut. Always diluted, on a pump.
  • Usually no faster than 10 mEq/hr peripherally (faster only per policy with central line + monitor).
  • Confirm urine output ≥ 30 mL/hr before and during. Assess the site (it burns; phlebitis).

Treating hyperkalemia: protect → shift → remove. (1) IV calcium gluconate protects the heart (doesn't lower K). (2) Shift K into cells: IV regular insulin + dextrose (watch glucose), albuterol, bicarb if acidotic. (3) Remove it: loop diuretics, binders (sodium polystyrene sulfonate, patiromer), dialysis. Steps 1–2 buy time; only step 3 lowers total body K.

Like I'm 10

A flooded basement. Calcium is rubber boots (protects you, doesn't move water). Insulin sweeps the water into a corner (still in the house). Dialysis and binders are the pump that gets it out.

Calcium = nerve stability (the doorstop)

Hypocalcemia (< 9.0)

  • Thyroidectomy/parathyroidectomy, radical neck, hypoparathyroidism, kidney failure, low vitamin D, pancreatitis, massive transfusion (citrate), low Mg, alkalosis
  • Tingling (mouth, fingers), cramps, tetany, + Chvostek (tap Cheek), + Trousseau (BP cuff), hyperreflexia, laryngospasm/stridor, seizures; long QT
  • IV calcium slowly on a monitor (don't mix with bicarb); oral Ca + vitamin D; airway equipment at bedside after thyroid surgery

Hypercalcemia (> 10.5)

  • Cancer (bone mets, myeloma, PTH-like hormone), hyperparathyroidism, immobility, thiazides, excess vitamin D
  • "Stones, bones, groans, moans": kidney stones, bone pain, constipation/nausea, confusion; weakness, ↓ reflexes, polyuria; short QT
  • IV NS first, then loop diuretic (never thiazides); bisphosphonates, calcitonin; mobility; fluids; strain urine

Low albumin lowers total calcium without changing the active (ionized) calcium; check ionized or corrected Ca. Calcium and phosphorus sit on a seesaw.

Magnesium & phosphorus

  • Low Mg: alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, diuretics, diarrhea, NG suction, long-term PPIs, refeeding. Looks like low Ca: tremors, hyperreflexia, tetany, Chvostek/Trousseau, seizures, torsades. Makes the kidneys waste K.
  • High Mg: kidney failure, Mg antacids/laxatives, IV magnesium sulfate. Flushing, ↓/absent DTRs, lethargy, hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression.
  • IV magnesium sulfate: check DTRs, RR, UO, LOC before and during. Stop and notify if DTRs are absent, RR < 12, or UO < 30 mL/hr. Antidote: IV calcium gluconate.
  • High phosphorus: kidney failure, TLS, phosphate enemas → causes low Ca; binders with meals. Low phosphorus: refeeding, alcohol, DKA treatment → weakness, respiratory failure (phosphorus is part of ATP). Start nutrition slowly in malnourished clients.

Acid-base & ABGs

Your Acid-Base & ABGs What & Why guide · Hurst workbook pp. 13–20

Normal: pH 7.35–7.45 · PaCO₂ 35–45 (lungs) · HCO₃⁻ 22–26 (kidneys) · PaO₂ 80–100, SaO₂ 95–100% (oxygenation, not acid-base).

  • CO₂ is acid in disguise: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. More CO₂ → more H⁺ → acidosis. Every breath out removes acid.
  • Three regulators: buffers (seconds, small capacity) → lungs (minutes to hours) → kidneys (hours to days, strongest). That's why acute respiratory problems look uncompensated and COPD is often fully compensated.
Like I'm 10

A leak in your house. Buffers are towels (instant but fill up fast). The lungs are a shop vac (quick). The kidneys are the plumber (takes days to show up, but actually fixes it).

ROME and the tic-tac-toe method

Respiratory Opposite (pH and PaCO₂ move opposite ways) · Metabolic Equal (pH and HCO₃⁻ move the same way).

  1. Label pH: acid (< 7.35), normal, base (> 7.45).
  2. Label PaCO₂: acid (> 45), normal, base (< 35). High CO₂ = acid.
  3. Label HCO₃⁻: acid (< 22), normal, base (> 26).
  4. Whichever matches the pH is the cause.
  5. Compensation: other value normal = uncompensated; other value opposite + pH abnormal = partially; opposite + pH normal = fully.
  6. Check oxygenation separately.

Normal pH but abnormal numbers? Use 7.40 as the tiebreaker: 7.35–7.39 leans acid, 7.41–7.45 leans alkaline. The body never overcompensates. Both values pointing the same way as the pH = mixed disorder (e.g., cardiac arrest: 7.18/58/17), the most dangerous kind.

7.32 / 50 / 29 → acid · acid · base → respiratory acidosis, partially compensated 7.32 / 41 / 20 → acid · normal · acid → metabolic acidosis, uncompensated 7.45 / 52 / 35 → normal-alkaline · acid · base → metabolic alkalosis, fully compensated 7.18 / 58 / 17 → acid · acid · acid → mixed respiratory + metabolic acidosis

Red flags & strategies
  • A COPD client who gets quiet and drowsy and stops complaining of dyspnea may be retaining CO₂, not improving.
  • A DKA client whose breathing suddenly slows while still acidotic may be tiring out.
  • Restlessness: think hypoxia first (Hurst). ↑ CO₂ = ↓ LOC.

Potassium-hydrogen shift

Acidosis → hyperkalemia; alkalosis → hypokalemia. In acidosis H⁺ moves into cells and K⁺ moves out (a revolving door). Correcting acidosis (insulin in DKA) drives K back in, so serum K can drop fast: check K frequently. Burns: early lactic acidosis + cell damage = two sources of high K.

Anion gap = Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻), normal ≈ 8–12. High gap = acid added (DKA, lactic, renal failure, toxins). Normal gap = bicarb lost (diarrhea).

ABG collection: Allen test before a radial stick; firm pressure ≥ 5 min (10+ on anticoagulants); remove air bubbles; label O₂ and temperature; ice if delayed; wait 20–30 min after O₂ changes or suctioning. In CO poisoning pulse ox reads falsely normal: you need an ABG with co-oximetry.

Burns

Your Burns What & Why guide and Iggy study guide (Ch 21) · Hurst workbook pp. 21–35

The big rule

Almost every burn question is about airway, fluid, or infection. Find the time since injury first: early = airway and fluid; after ~72 hours = infection, fluid overload, contractures.

Skin, types, depth

  • Skin is the raincoat: barrier to infection, holds fluid, regulates temperature, sensation, vitamin D, appearance. Each lost job = a nursing problem.
  • Types: thermal (most common), chemical (alkalis penetrate deeper, keep burning longer), electrical, inhalation, radiation, cold. In young children, "stocking/glove" scald lines or burns that don't fit the story suggest abuse: mandatory report.

Zones of injury: coagulation (center, dead) → stasis (savable, or lost to hypovolemia, hypothermia, ice, infection) → hyperemia (recovers). The burn can deepen over 2–3 days, so depth and TBSA are reassessed on days 2–3.

Like I'm 10

A lawn. Superficial: grass scorched, roots fine, grows back fast. Partial: some roots survive. Full thickness: roots and soil burned away, so nothing grows back unless you lay new sod (a graft).

Sizing the burn and who needs a burn center

TBSA

  • Rule of nines (adult): head & neck 9 · each arm 9 · anterior trunk 18 · posterior trunk 18 · each leg 18 (front 9/back 9) · perineum 1
  • Front of one arm 4.5%; front of one leg 9%
  • Palm with fingers ≈ 1% (scattered burns). Lund-Browder: most precise, preferred for children
  • Only partial and full thickness count

Burn center referral (ABA)

  • Partial thickness > 10% TBSA; any full thickness
  • Face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum, major joints
  • Electrical, chemical, or inhalation injury
  • Serious pre-existing illness or major trauma; children without pediatric capability

Emergent phase: burn shock

Mediators open gaps in capillaries (systemic if > ~20–25% TBSA). Albumin escapes, oncotic pull drops, and the leak feeds itself. Greatest in the first ~8–12 hours, continuing to ~24.

Airway, inhalation injury, carbon monoxide

  • Suspect with an enclosed space, facial burns, singed nasal hair/eyebrows, soot or carbonaceous sputum, hoarseness/voice change, stridor, drooling, trouble swallowing, wheezing.
  • Upper airway (heat) swells fast, often within 24–48 h, and worsens once big fluid volumes start. Lower airway (chemicals) → bronchospasm, pneumonia, ARDS at 24–72 h.
  • 100% O₂ by nonrebreather, HOB up (no spine injury), anticipate early intubation. Hoarseness or stridor outranks fluid and pain.
  • CO binds Hgb 200+ times more strongly than O₂. Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion → seizures, coma. Cherry-red skin is late and unreliable. Pulse ox reads falsely normal; diagnose with COHb (co-oximetry). Treat with 100% O₂ (hyperbaric in some cases).
Like I'm 10

CO takes every seat on the oxygen bus and won't get up. The bus looks full (SpO₂ 98%), but none of the passengers are oxygen.

Scene and ED care

  • Your safety first; stop the burning (stop, drop, roll; remove from source; remove burned clothing unless stuck). Fire in a facility: RACE (Rescue, Alarm, Confine, Evacuate); extinguisher PASS.
  • ABCDE with c-spine protection for trauma, falls, electrical. Remove all jewelry early (becomes a tourniquet).
  • Cool small burns with cool running water about 10–20 min (Hurst says no more than 10). No ice, butter, ointments. Cover with a clean, dry sheet; large burns: limit cooling, keep warm.
  • Two large-bore IVs (> ~20% TBSA), indwelling catheter for hourly UO, NG tube for burns ≥ 25% or intubated clients. Tetanus if not current (no dose in 10 years or unknown).
  • Check apical/carotid/femoral pulses; sterile dressing under a BP cuff on burned skin; cardiac monitor; elevate burned limbs; eye consult for facial burns. Labs: CBC, lytes, BUN/Cr, glucose, ABG with COHb, CPK, albumin, urinalysis for myoglobin, CXR, ECG.

Fluid resuscitation

Parkland (your guides, Iggy): 4 mL LR × kg × %TBSA = first 24 h ½ in the first 8 h from the TIME OF BURN · ½ over the next 16 h Superficial burns are NOT counted. Example: 80 kg, 30% → 4 × 80 × 30 = 9,600 mL first 8 h: 4,800 mL = 600 mL/h · next 16 h: 4,800 mL = 300 mL/h Late start: burned 0900, IV at 1100 → first half runs over 6 h (800 mL/h)
Your Hurst workbook differs

The workbook (p. 27) teaches the ABA range 2–4 mL, using 2 mL for adult thermal, 3 mL for children, 4 mL for electrical. Your guides say to use the number your textbook and instructor use. If a question gives a multiplier, use it.

  • LR is preferred (closer to plasma; lactate → bicarb helps acidosis; large NS volumes cause hyperchloremic acidosis). No boluses. Colloids (albumin) are held until capillaries start to seal (~12–24 h), or they leak out too.
  • Hourly urine output is the #1 indicator: adults 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr (30–50 mL/hr) · electrical/myoglobin 75–100 mL/hr · children ~1 mL/kg/hr. Titrate the rate to UO; report UO below target for 2 hours in a row.
  • Too little → AKI, shock, deepening burns. Too much ("fluid creep") → pulmonary edema, compartment syndrome, worse airway edema.

Circumferential burns

Eschar doesn't stretch, so swelling under it crushes veins, then arteries and nerves. Check the 6 P's: pain (out of proportion, on passive stretch), pallor, paresthesia, pulselessness (late), paralysis, poikilothermia. Chest burns restrict ventilation. Treat with escharotomy or fasciotomy. Elevate, hourly neurovascular checks (Doppler). Rising pain, numbness, or lost Doppler pulse → notify now.

Chemical & electrical

Chemical

  • Brush off dry powder first, then irrigate with large amounts of water (20+ min, longer for alkalis; eyes prolonged)
  • Remove contaminated clothing; wear PPE
  • Don't neutralize (the reaction releases heat → second burn)

Electrical

  • Entry/exit wounds; damage follows the current through deep tissue (iceberg)
  • Dysrhythmias (V-fib, asystole) → cardiac monitoring ~24 h
  • Rhabdomyolysis → tea-colored urine (myoglobin) → AKI; higher UO target; monitor CK and K
  • Compartment syndrome; c-spine precautions (falls, thrown); fractures from contractions

Hurst adds: for myoglobinuria, mannitol (inline filter, check clarity, don't refrigerate) and more fluids until the urine clears.

Acute phase: infection, wounds, grafts

  • Infection/sepsis is the leading cause of death after the emergent phase. Signs are subtle (baseline temp and HR are already high): confusion, tachypnea, hypo- or hyperthermia, new hyperglycemia, feeding intolerance, thrombocytopenia, ileus, wound changes (partial converting to full thickness, foul drainage, early eschar separation, graft sloughing).
  • Prevention: hand hygiene, aseptic wound care (cap, gown, mask, gloves), protective environment, no plants/flowers, early wound closure, early enteral feeding. Premedicate ~30 min before dressing changes; keep the room warm.

  • Grafts: autograft (own skin, permanent; split- or full-thickness); allograft (cadaver) and xenograft (pig) are temporary with higher infection risk. A graft lives on the wound bed until capillaries grow in (~3–5 days): immobilize, elevate, no BP cuff on the grafted limb, avoid shear, watch for fluid under the graft and infection. The donor site is a new partial-thickness wound, often more painful.
  • Pain: IV opioids in the emergent phase (IM/subcut absorb erratically, then flood in when fluid shifts back). Premedicate for procedures. Itching is common in healing wounds.
  • GI: paralytic ileus (NG to suction); Curling's ulcer (risk ↑ with ventilation, multiorgan problems, > 30% TBSA) → PPI or H₂ blocker + early enteral feeding.
  • Nutrition: hypermetabolic (metabolic rate can double): high calorie, high protein (2–4× normal), high carbohydrate; start enteral feeding early (often within 24 h). If a question says NPO until bowel sounds return, returning bowel sounds mean the ileus has resolved.
  • Temperature: burned skin can't hold heat; warm room, warmed fluids, cover wounds.

Rehabilitative phase

  • The position of comfort is the position of contracture. ROM several times a day, splints, positioning in extension. Neck: no pillows (small towel under shoulders). Axilla: arms abducted ~90°. Hands: splinted in a functional position (Hurst: wrap fingers separately).
  • Hypertrophic scars: pressure garments ~23 hours a day for a year or more. Protect healed skin from the sun.
  • Psychosocial: body image, anxiety, depression, PTSD, grief, guilt. Assess readiness before encouraging the client to look at scars; demonstrate acceptance; support groups.
  • Prevention teaching: no smoking in the home (never near home O₂), working smoke detectors, two ways out of every room, fire extinguisher, water heater ≤ 120°F (49°C), pot handles in, outlet covers.

Oncology

Your Oncology What & Why guide and Iggy study guide (Ch 18) · Hurst workbook pp. 36–54

How cancer happens

  • Normal cells stop when crowded and self-destruct when damaged. Cancer = DNA mutations: proto-oncogenes become oncogenes (gas pedal stuck on) and tumor suppressor genes like p53 fail (brakes cut). Usually needs several mutations, which is why risk rises with age.
  • Benign: encapsulated, well-differentiated, slow, doesn't metastasize (but can still kill by location, e.g., brain). Malignant: not encapsulated, poorly differentiated, invades, metastasizes, recurs.
  • Carcinogenesis: initiation (irreversible mutation) → promotion (tobacco, alcohol, obesity, hormones make mutated cells multiply; reversible if exposure stops) → progression (invasion, metastasis). That's why quitting smoking helps at any stage.
  • Metastasis: lymphatic (most common for carcinomas), bloodstream (lungs, liver, bone, brain), seeding into a cavity. Named for the origin: breast cancer in bone = metastatic breast cancer.
  • Grading (I–IV) = how abnormal the cells look. Staging (TNM) = how far it has spread. Higher is worse for both.
Like I'm 10

Initiation is dropping a lit match (can't un-drop it). Promotion is pouring gasoline on it: stop pouring and it may burn out. Progression is the fire jumping to the next house.

Risk, prevention, screening

  • Tobacco is the #1 preventable cause (≥ 30% of cancers); obesity/inactivity next; aging is the biggest overall risk factor (Hurst workbook).
  • Primary (is the cancer there yet? no): smoking cessation, sunscreen, HPV and hepatitis B vaccines, diet, exercise, limit alcohol. Secondary (looking for it): screening. Tertiary (already treated): rehab, managing effects, support groups.
  • CAUTION: Change in bowel/bladder · A sore that doesn't heal · Unusual bleeding/discharge · Thickening or lump · Indigestion or trouble swallowing · Obvious change in a wart or mole · Nagging cough or hoarseness. Also unexplained weight loss, fatigue, fever.

Chemotherapy

  • Kills dividing cells, including hair, GI lining, and marrow (the side-effect list). Cell-cycle specific (antimetabolites in S phase, vinca alkaloids in M phase) vs nonspecific (alkylating agents). Given in combinations and cycles so marrow can recover.
  • Safe handling: double chemo-rated gloves, impermeable gown, eye/face protection if splash risk; prepared in a biologic safety cabinet. Excreta can contain drug for about 48 hours: PPE for body fluids, double-flush per policy. (Hurst workbook says 3–7 days; go with your textbook.) Disposal in yellow chemo containers.
  • Extravasation (pain, burning, swelling, no blood return): stop the infusion, leave the catheter in and aspirate residual drug, notify provider, antidote per protocol, cold compress for most drugs (warm for vinca alkaloids), elevate, document. Prevent with blood-return checks; central lines preferred for vesicants. Don't "wait and see."
  • Other: targeted therapy, immunotherapy (checkpoint inhibitors → immune-related colitis, pneumonitis, thyroiditis, rash), hormonal therapy (tamoxifen), stem cell transplant (profound infection risk until engraftment).

Science, theory, and the words

  • Science brings facts together and gives them coherence. Scientific process: hypothesis → method → data collection → results → evaluation.
  • Theory: abstract generalization that explains how phenomena relate; needs at least two related concepts. Theory guides research; research validates theory.
  • Concept = basic ingredient (pain, stress, adaptation). Proposition = statement of the relationship between concepts. Conceptual model/framework = concepts grouped by a common theme (looser than a theory). Schematic model = a diagram (blueprint) of the theory. Philosophy = values and beliefs (Watson).
  • Metaparadigm (the four concepts in nursing models): nursing, person, health, environment.
  • Levels: grand theory = broad (Rogers' unitary man). Middle-range/practice theory = specific population or situation (Beck's postpartum depression, Pender's Health Promotion, theory of unpleasant symptoms, Mishel). Nurses use middle-range theories daily.

RelevanceDoes it reflect the scientific base it came from?

In this textbook, "can someone replicate it?" is Simplicity, not Accuracy or Consistency. Your peer's guide gets this one wrong both ways.

Nightingale: the first nursing theorist (environmental adaptation)

Nursing puts the patient in the best condition for nature to act. Her canons: ventilation and warming (air as pure as outside without chilling) · noise (no sudden noise, chatter, or whispering) · variety (flowers, colors, pets) · diet · light (second only to fresh air) · chattering hopes and advices (don't cheer the sick by making light of their danger: false hope) · cleanliness (health of houses; hand washing with friction).

Ch 8 · Legal issues in nursing and health care

Course objectives 2, 3 & 8 · SLO 6

Where law comes from

  • Laws say "must/shall" (minimum conduct with penalties). Ethics say "should/may."
  • Statutory law: written by legislatures (Congress, state). Violations are crimes. Ignorance of the law is never a defense.
  • Common law (case law, judge-made): appellate court opinions that set the standard of nursing conduct.
  • Civil law (torts) vs criminal law (offenses against society; misdemeanors and felonies).

Federal statutes

LawWhat it does
Medicare Conditions of Participation (42 CFR 482)Minimum standards: individualized written care plan, trained staff, adequate staffing, discharge planning ASAP. CMS won't pay for "never events" (hospital-acquired conditions): retained foreign object, air embolism, blood incompatibility, stage III/IV pressure ulcer, falls and trauma, poor glycemic control, CAUTI, vascular catheter infection, certain SSIs, DVT/PE after some surgeries
EMTALA (1986)"Antidumping": everyone gets a medical screening exam and is stabilized regardless of ability to pay; no transfer of unstable patients or women in labor. Fines $25–50K
ADA (1990)Reasonable accommodation: interpreters, assistive devices (e.g., head-activated call light for a quadriplegic client)
Patient Self-Determination Act (1990)On admission, written notice of the right to participate in decisions, accept or refuse treatment, and make advance directives; ask if the client has one and note it
HIPAA (1996)Confidentiality of health information; whistle-blower protection for good-faith reports. Social media posts of patients (even "unidentifiable" body parts) are forbidden
Patient Safety & Quality Improvement Act (2005)Allows non-identifiable safety data to go to patient safety organizations
False Claims ActOffense to submit false Medicare/Medicaid claims; whistle-blower gets 15–25% of recovery

State law

  • Nurse Practice Act: defines RN, scope, standards, misconduct, grounds for discipline. Board rules have the force of law. Clarify any order that is inaccurate, unauthorized, not current, harmful, or contraindicated.
  • Board discipline: reprimand, probation, fines, limit/suspend/revoke license. Most discipline is tied to chemical impairment (10–15% of RNs chemically dependent).
  • Staffing: California (1999, effective 2004) was the only state with mandated nurse-patient ratios. Many states restrict mandatory overtime. Corporate negligence claims for understaffing.
  • Reporting statutes: child abuse (immunity for good-faith reports), elder abuse, domestic violence (some states), communicable diseases, weapon injuries. Failing to report can bring criminal charges.

Negligence and malpractice (unintentional torts)

Negligence: failure to act as a reasonably prudent person would. Malpractice: negligence by a professional; usually needs an expert witness.

4 elements the plaintiff must prove

  1. Duty: nurse-patient relationship (accepting the assignment; phone triage counts)
  2. Breach of the standard of care
  3. Actual harm/damage
  4. Proximate cause: direct link (or "substantial cause")

Special doctrines

  • Res ipsa loquitur: "the thing speaks for itself" (retained sponge); no expert needed
  • Gross negligence: reckless disregard → punitive damages
  • Criminal negligence: so reckless it's a crime (10× penicillin dose to a newborn; epidural given IV)
  • Comparative negligence: damages split by each defendant's share (nurses paid 15% in Janga)
  • Affirmative duty (Utter v. United Hospital, 1977): nurses must use independent judgment and take positive action, including the chain of command. "I was following orders" is never a defense.
  • Students: usually ordinary negligence; the supervising instructor/nurse and the hospital (vicarious liability) can be named. The hospital is held to the same standard as for a licensed nurse's error. Tell patients a student is caring for them.
  • Most frequent allegations: failure to communicate/report (the #1 cause of error per TJC), failure to monitor, ensure safety, rescue; improper treatment; medication errors; not following policy; not invoking the chain of command.

Defenses and limits

  • Emergency: reasonable deviation to save a life (skipping a full prep in a crash C-section).
  • Governmental immunity: VA, USPHS, NASA, DoD nurses; the government is substituted as defendant.
  • Good Samaritan: off-duty emergency help; plaintiff must prove intentional harm or gross negligence. Stopping creates a nurse-patient relationship. Some states have "duty to rescue" laws.
  • Statute of limitations: often 2–3 years; extended by the discovery rule, fraud or concealment, retained objects, incompetence, minors.
  • Disclosure of errors leads to less severe consequences. The provider leads disclosure; the nurse shouldn't disclose before talking with the provider and leaders.
  • Nurses should carry their own malpractice insurance (expanding roles, floating, supervising, employers not defending, coverage limits).

Liability

TypeMeaning
PersonalYou're always accountable for your own acts; no one can take it for you ("I'll take responsibility")
FloatingDon't perform tasks you lack skill for. Negotiate; ask for a resource nurse and document who will co-manage. Winkelman: a nurse may refuse an unsafe float. Francis: firing upheld when the nurse refused offered orientation

Risk management & incident reports

  • Report every unusual occurrence (injury, unexpected death, equipment failure, adverse reactions, unmet needs, refusals, unsafe practice, complaints, spills, violence). Usually to risk management within 24 h.
  • Write objectively: "Patient discovered lying prone at the foot of the bed," not "patient fell out of bed."
  • Never mention the incident report in the chart, photocopy it, or let a provider "order" one.

Intentional torts

TortExample
AssaultThreat that causes fear of unconsented touching ("stop screaming or I'll inject you")

Patient rights

  • Living will: wishes for end-of-life care; written, dated, signed, witnessed. If asked to witness, refer to risk management (may be unlawful for you). Can be revoked at any time.
  • DNR: a lawfully executed DNR must be followed, even if family objects; get the supervisor. Ignoring it → battery, "wrongful life."
  • Durable power of attorney for health care: names the proxy who decides once the client is decisionally incompetent.
  • Informed consent: the provider discloses nature, benefits, risks, alternatives, and risks of refusing. Not delegable to the RN. If the client doesn't understand, notify the provider and delay; hold your witness signature. If a surgeon starts a procedure the consent doesn't cover (debridement → amputation), question it, don't open the instruments, and get the supervisor.
  • Interpreters: qualified; family or friends only at the client's request. Deaf clients: ASL interpreter. Document the interpreter's name. All states let minors consent to STD testing/treatment.
  • Refusal/AMA: competent adults may refuse (Jehovah's Witness case). Notify the provider; explain dangers; document; never detain.
  • Restraints: least restrictive, last resort, never for short staffing. Bedrails and chair trays count. Nurses may restrain in an emergency, then contact the provider promptly. Orders timed and dated; document assessments.
Like I'm 10

To win a negligence case you need all four legs of the table: a duty, breaking it, someone got hurt, and your mistake caused the hurt. Take away any leg and the table falls over. Intentional torts are different: doing the thing on purpose is enough, even with no injury.

Ch 10 · Cultural competency and social issues

Course objective 5 · SLO 3, 7

Terms

  • Culture: shared values, beliefs, practices passed down. Ethnicity: shared linguistic, racial, or cultural background. Minority: group smaller than the majority.
  • Ethnocentric: my culture is best. Prejudice: preconceived negative judgment. Stereotyping: assigning beliefs to a group without seeing the individual.
  • Cultural humility: lifelong self-evaluation, fixing power imbalances, partnerships. Cultural sensitivity: neutral language that shows appreciation. Transcultural: grounded in your own culture, skilled in others.

The numbers

  • Federally defined minority groups: Asian American · Black/African American · Hispanic/Latino · Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander · American Indian/Alaska Native. Minorities projected to be the majority by 2044.
  • Adults 65+ will double to about 72 million; ~20% of the population by 2030.
  • Highest poverty: American Indian/Alaska Native, then Black. Highest uninsured: Hispanic.
  • Homicide is the 2nd leading cause of death at ages 15–24. Intimate partner violence is the single greatest cause of injury to women 15–24, across all groups.
  • RN workforce: about 83% white, 6% Black, 6% Asian, 3% Hispanic (2013 survey); men about 11% of RNs.
  • History: NACGN (1908) recruited Black nurses before WWII; Cadet Nurse Corps in WWII; Brown v. Board (1954); Chi Eta Phi mentors.

Attitude continuum

Hate → contempt → tolerance (minimum needed in a democracy) → respect → celebration (most positive).

Interpreters and CLAS

  • A skilled interpreter is mandatory. No children (subject matter, authority). Consider gender, birth origin/dialect, and socioeconomic class; medical-terminology skills matter. Some groups see a stranger as a breach of confidentiality; others don't want family to know.
  • CLAS (Office of Minority Health): free interpreter services for LEP clients, notices of that right in their language, translated signage and materials, competent interpreters (family and friends aren't adequate substitutes), record primary language, ongoing organizational self-assessments, grievance processes, annual progress report.

Cultural assessment

  1. Self-assessment first: your own attitudes and ethnocentric tendencies.
  2. Client assessment: ethnic affiliation, religion, family patterns, food patterns (what counts as "food"), health practices.
  3. Classify each practice: adaptive (beneficial), neutral, or maladaptive (harmful). Garlic instead of the antihypertensive = maladaptive; garlic with it = neutral; garlic + med + daily exercise = adaptive.
  4. Dermal practices (cupping, pinching, coining, burning) leave marks. Usually adaptive or neutral; maladaptive with a clotting disorder. Differentiate from abuse.

Healers and extended family can be part of the plan if not harmful; give them privacy. Illnesses outside Western medicine: mal ojo, susto, bilis, empacho.

Like I'm 10

Before you can understand someone else's "normal," you have to notice your own. Then ask, don't assume, and keep anything they do that helps or doesn't hurt.

Transition shock (Duchscher)

  • Abrupt shock of moving from student to nurse: doubt, confusion, disorientation, loss.
  • Elements: stability (ask: how many nurses left last year? staffing? acuity?), predictability, familiarity, consistency.
  • Takes about 6 months to find your "sea legs." Stages: doing → being → knowing.
  • 25% of new nurses leave within the first year (NCSBN).

Why it's a shock

  • School = whole-task (primary nursing, holistic). Work often = partial-task/functional nursing (tasks divided; checkmark = quality check).
  • School evaluates the correct steps; work evaluates whether care met policy and procedure.

Benner: novice to expert (1984)

  1. Novice: rules learned by rote (still in school)
  2. Advanced beginner: most new nurses enter here
  3. Competent: long-range goals, mastering skills
  4. Proficient: sees the whole situation
  5. Expert: intuition; instant decisions

Intuition is nonconscious pattern recognition, not guessing; it develops with experience and sits beside objective data.

Special needs of novice nurses

Interpersonal, clinical, organizational, delegation, priority-setting, and assertiveness skills; dealing with horizontal violence; intuition. Practice what you'll say before clarifying an order.

Workplace violence

  • Type 1 criminal stranger · Type 2 patient/client · Type 3 coworker (horizontal/lateral violence) · Type 4 personal relationship.
  • Horizontal violence: withholding patient information, exclusion, belittling, refusing to help, eye-rolling, scapegoating. ANA: zero tolerance.

Strategies to ease transition

  • NCSBN Transition to Practice model: same preceptor for 6 months (+ support for 6 more), built on QSEN modules.
  • Biculturalism: merging school values with workplace values.
  • Mentor: voluntary, two-way, ongoing relationship (at least 2 years) with an experienced nurse who guides, supports, and validates intuition; recipient = protégé. Role model: an example to watch. Preceptor: experienced nurse who orients you to a specific unit and teaches skills (final semester or orientation). Self-mentoring: your own resources, problem solving, questions.
  • Residency: usually 12 months; Vizient/AACN program ~95% first-year retention vs ~75% national.
  • Join NSNA/ANA. Watch for impostor feelings; build self-esteem.
  • NURSES: Never fail to ask for help · Use facility resources · Reenergize with professional associations · Stay in contact with friends · Evaluate your growth realistically · Stay focused on goals.
  • Job hunting: first-year turnover over 20% is a concern.
Like I'm 10

A preceptor is a driving instructor in the passenger seat for your first months on one road. A mentor is the coach you keep for years who helps you figure out where you want to drive.

Morgenstern's 10 psychological obstacles

Unclear goals · conquistador of chaos (crisis manager) · fear of downtime · need to be a caretaker · fear of failure · fear of success · fear of disrupting the status quo · fear of completion · need for perfection · fear of losing creativity.

Distractions

External

  • Interruptions, visitors, meetings
  • Paperwork, understaffing, lack of information
  • Poor communication, unclear authority, red tape, waiting

Internal (from you)

  • Procrastination, poor planning, poor delegation
  • Inability to say no, attempting too much
  • Clutter, disorganization, indecision, daydreaming, haste, surfing the Internet

Plan → organize → implement

  • Planning is the most important step. 1 minute of planning ≈ 10 minutes of productivity; arrive 10–15 minutes early to map priorities.
  • Priorities: urgent (now) vs important (flexible timing). ABC: A = high value, B = medium, C = low (do A first; some C's never need doing). Pareto (80/20): 20% of effort produces 80% of results; the vital few. Continuum: intrinsic importance, urgency, delegation, visitations.
  • Parkinson's law: work expands to fill the time available; scheduling controls it. 15 focused minutes ≈ 1 hour of productivity. Schedule tasks in your "prime time."
  • To-do list: daily, revised as priorities shift, reviewed at the end of the shift.
  • Organizing: clear the stacked desk (and mind) · no detourism (one task to completion) · wastebasketry (handle a paper once: do it, delegate it, dump it) · professional e-mail.
  • Implementing: attack priorities early; break big tasks down. Procrastination: recognize it, admit it, then identify the task, ask why, consider delegating, set deadlines, one piece at a time, 95% can be enough (never in patient safety). Delegation steps (the delegator keeps accountability). Control interruptions (focused phone greeting). Say "no" clearly and briefly without an elaborate excuse. Reward yourself. Use technology.
  • When overwhelmed: stop and plan. Ask, "What is the best use of my time right now?"

Ch 28 · The NCLEX-RN examination

Course objective 9 · textbook + 2026 NCLEX-RN Test Plan (current rules)

Purpose

(1) Safeguard the public from unsafe practitioners; (2) help boards of nursing decide who can perform entry-level RN work. Graduates of associate, diploma, and baccalaureate programs all take the same exam. Pass/fail. Given by computer adaptive testing (CAT) at Pearson VUE.

How the exam works now (2026 Test Plan)

RuleDetail
LengthMinimum 85, maximum 150 items, 5 hours including breaks
Minimum-length exam52 content items + 18 case-study items (3 case studies × 6) + 15 unscored pretest items
Clinical judgment3 case studies plus about 10% stand-alone clinical judgment items (bowtie, trend)
CATEach item is chosen so you have about a 50% chance of answering correctly. Competency depends on the difficulty of items you answer correctly, not how many. No skipping, no going back
Pass/fail rules(1) 95% confidence interval rule (most common) · (2) maximum-length: final ability estimate decides · (3) run out of time: below minimum items = fail; otherwise final ability estimate from all completed items
ScoringPartial credit on multi-key items: plus/minus, 0/1, and rationale scoring
Outdated numbers in the textbook

The 8th-edition chapter still says 75–265 items, 6 hours, and "analyze the last 60 questions" if time runs out. Those rules ended with the Next Generation NCLEX. Use the 2026 numbers above.

Clinical Judgment Measurement Model (6 steps)

  1. Recognize cues: pick out relevant data (history, vital signs)
  2. Analyze cues: connect the cues to the client's condition
  3. Prioritize hypotheses: rank explanations by urgency, likelihood, risk
  4. Generate solutions: expected outcomes and possible interventions
  5. Take action: do the highest-priority solution
  6. Evaluate outcomes: compare what happened with what you expected

Your instructor's slide says "Picturize hypotheses." The exam term is Prioritize hypotheses.

Bloom's levels

Remember (normal K range) → understand (which item fits a clear liquid diet) → apply (priority assessment before IM morphine) → analyze (tetany + Trousseau + Ca 8 → hypocalcemia; setting priorities, drawing conclusions). Most NCLEX items are at application or higher.

Build your study plan

Read the candidate bulletin and test plan · get your Authorization to Test (apply to your board first) · needs assessment of strengths and weaknesses · analyze standardized exams · pick individual vs group study · study in ~50-minute blocks · learn concepts, not isolated facts · practice questions and read every rationale · about 1 minute per item · avoid excess caffeine · stay positive: the goal is minimum entry-level competence. Most students study 2–3 months.

Like I'm 10

The NCLEX is an eye exam. It keeps flipping lenses until it's sure. Stopping early doesn't mean you failed or passed; it means the machine is sure. Getting hard questions right counts more than getting lots of easy ones right.
