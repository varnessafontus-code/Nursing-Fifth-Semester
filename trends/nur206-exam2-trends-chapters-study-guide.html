Delegation and supervision

Cherry Ch 20 · LaCharity Ch 1

Key terms

  • Delegation Handing off a task while the registered nurse (RN) keeps accountability for the outcome. The nursing process and judgment are never delegated.
  • Assignment Dividing the shift's work within each person's job description and scope.
  • Supervision Guiding, checking, following up, and correcting. Delegating always means supervising.
  • Accountability Registered nurse (RN): decides what to delegate, stays available, evaluates the outcome. Delegatee: accepts only tasks they're competent in, does them, reports back.

Five rights of delegation

RightAsk yourself
TaskIs it allowed for this person by law and policy?
CircumstancesIs the patient stable? Does it need no nursing judgment?
PersonIs their competence verified, not assumed?
Direction and communicationDid I say what, when, and which numbers to report?
Supervision and evaluationDid I follow up and check the result?

Who does what

Assistive personnel (AP)Licensed practical nurse (LPN)Registered nurse (RN) only
Vital signs on stable patients · intake and output (I&O) · weights · activities of daily living (ADLs) · ambulating, turning · feeding (no swallow problem) · reminding · reporting a number By mouth (PO), subcutaneous (SubQ), intramuscular (IM) meds · dressings · data collection on stable patients · reinforcing teaching · tube feedings Admission and initial assessment · care plans · initial and discharge teaching · evaluating · unstable or new patients · intravenous (IV) push · blood · interpreting labs and electrocardiograms (ECGs)
  • Trap words Assistive personnel (AP) can remind, measure, report. AP cannot teach, assess, monitor, evaluate.
  • Supervision levels Unsupervised = registered nurse (RN) with an RN peer · Periodic = staff you know · Continuous = new staff, complex task, or unproven competence.
  • Assigning Float nurse → patients that match the home unit's skills. New graduate → stable, predictable patients. Pregnant nurse → no airborne illness (tuberculosis (TB), varicella, measles).
  • NCSBN process Assess and plan → communicate → surveil and supervise → evaluate and give feedback.

Kid version

Delegation is a coach sending a player in. The player runs the play, but the coach still owns the score, and the coach has to watch the game.

Ch 17 · Leadership and management

Cherry pp. 714–760

Leader vs. manager

  • Leader Influences and guides people. May have no title.
  • Manager Coordinates resources (time, people, supplies) to reach outcomes. Has an appointed position and formal authority.
  • Authority vs. power Authority = the right to direct, given by the organization. Power = the ability to get things done.
  • Problem solving First step in both the nursing process and problem solving = gather information.

Management functions, in order

  1. Planning: goals and policies. Strategic plan a written plan 3–5 years out (e.g., a 5-year plan to add clinical nurse leaders).
  2. Organizing: structure and resources.
  3. Staffing: hiring, scheduling, orienting.
  4. Directing: leading, motivating, delegating.
  5. Controlling: performance reviews, budget review, quality data.

Change and motivation

  • Lewin's change Unfreeze (make people see the need for change, e.g., a handout showing the current plan's problems) → Move (try the new way) → Refreeze (reinforce until it's routine, e.g., spot checks that everyone does the new rounds).
  • Positive reinforcement Specific, right after the achievement, spontaneous (routine praise loses value), and earned.
  • Informal leader An upset, experienced nurse: acknowledge their expertise and state clear expectations for teamwork. Don't exclude or transfer them.
  • Manager roles Customer service provider, team builder, resource manager, change agent, clinical consultant, staff developer, mentor, corporate supporter (works toward the organization's mission).
  • Internal customers Employees at every level: x-ray tech, pharmacist, chief financial officer. Insurers and accreditors are external.

Kid version

The leader is the kid everyone follows because of a great game idea. The manager holds the clipboard and makes sure there are enough balls. Changing a habit is like an ice cube: melt it (unfreeze), reshape it (move), freeze it again (refreeze).

Ch 18 · Budgeting basics

Cherry pp. 763–787
  • Budget The financial plan for expected expenses and revenue, usually 12 months.
  • Functions Planning · communicating and coordinating (managers raise resource concerns with leaders) · monitoring · evaluating.

Types of budgets

BudgetCoversExample
OperatingDaily revenue and expensesSupplies, utilities, repairs
LaborSalaries, overtime, benefits (largest expense)Overtime to cover census
CapitalLasts over 1 year AND costs over a set minimumReplacing 50 beds with fall-sensor beds; monitors

How budgets get built

Approach / methodWhat happens
Top-downAdministrators set the goals; managers don't contribute.
ParticipatoryThe people responsible help set the goals (staff goals taken to upper management).
IterativeLeaders set strategic goals; units build budgets to meet them (80% Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) goal → tuition reimbursement).
IncrementalLast year's numbers trended forward (most common).
Zero-basedStart from zero and justify everything; no outdated data carried over.

Reading the results

  • Budget assumptions Predictions the budget rests on: census, supply prices, salaries, competitors (e.g., two physician groups moving admissions here).
  • Variance Budgeted vs. actual. Favorable = better than planned; unfavorable = worse. Effectiveness = actual vs. expected performance.
  • Quantitative vs. qualitative Quantitative = the numbers. Qualitative = why: reconciling the original assumptions with current conditions (higher acuity, more admissions).
  • Productivity Hours worked compared with hours of care (120 hours worked to give 75 hours of care). Goal = balanced, not high or low. Used to monitor the labor budget.
  • Costs Fixed = same at any volume (manager salary, accreditation fees, medication-dispensing system). Variable = rises with volume (registered nurse (RN) staffing, supplies). Direct = patient care. Indirect = overhead.
  • Nurse's role Managers are judged on overtime and supply variances. Getting staff on the budget committee builds ownership. New managers: read financial policy, talk with the finance office, learn from other managers.
  • Formulas 1 full-time equivalent (FTE) = 40 hours a week = 2,080 hours a year · hours per patient-day (HPPD) = nursing hours ÷ patient-days · Variance = budgeted − actual.

Kid version

Operating budget = groceries and the electric bill. Capital budget = saving for a car. Fixed costs are rent (same no matter what); variable costs are pizza (more people, more pizza).

Ch 19 · Communication and conflict

Cherry pp. 789–839

How communication works

  • Parts Sender, receiver, message. Feedback = the receiver becomes the sender. Spoken word is the most common form.
  • What shapes it Preconceived ideas (opinions formed before meeting) · past experiences (a nurse's own stillbirth affects her care) · filtration (tuning out extra stimuli) · perception.
  • Positive Active listening (undivided attention, eye contact, rephrasing, finish listening before speaking) · empathy (seeing it their way: "I understand how you could feel angry") · open-ended questions ("Tell me what day it is").
  • Negative Blocking (shrugging, noncommittal answers) · false assurance · closed yes/no questions · conflicting body language.

  • Supportive climate Supportiveness = everyone's opinion is valued and people are willing to change their position. Also empathy, equality, openness, positiveness.
  • Generations Generation X: grew up with high divorce rates, skeptical, values work-life balance and teamwork. Baby Boomers: work-centered. Traditionalists: loyal, respect authority. Millennials: tech-fluent, want frequent feedback.

Handoffs, email, and social media

  • SBAR Situation → Background → Assessment → Recommendation. In a code, use closed-loop communication.
  • Email Specific subject, short body with date, time, place, and one clear ask, then a signature with contact info.
  • Social media No names AND no identifiers (workplace, room, age plus diagnosis, "seen in the emergency department (ED) last night"). Deleted posts still exist. Online etiquette = same decency as in person; don't fan conflict; respect privacy and copyright.

Ch 21 · Staffing and care delivery models

Cherry pp. 874–902

Staffing

  • Staffing Making sure the right number and mix of staff are available (e.g., four registered nurses (RNs), five licensed practical nurses (LPNs), two unlicensed assistive personnel (UAPs) per shift).
  • First step Know the intensity of care patients need (physical and psychosocial).
  • Acuity The amount and intensity of care required. Patient classification systems measure it with criteria like fall risk, infection protocols, and communication needs; building one starts with patient traits (planned procedures, age, ethnicity).
  • Factors Census · complexity of care · unit layout and technology · staff education and experience. Not nurses' task preferences.
  • Regulators The Joint Commission (TJC) doesn't set ratios; it looks for evidence of satisfactory care. American Nurses Association (ANA): staffing must give registered nurses (RNs) time to use the nursing process.
  • Satisfaction Participatory staffing and autonomy keep nurses. Nurse satisfaction tracks with patient satisfaction. Patient satisfaction depends most on personal interactions with staff.

  • Clinical pathway A timeline of care and recovery milestones for one diagnosis. Falling off it (extra ventilator days after coronary artery bypass graft (CABG)) = a variance.
  • Future of care More minimally invasive procedures, fewer nurses for a growing older population, more focus on social and environmental influences and patient values.

Ch 15 · Information technology

Cherry pp. 651–678

Electronic health record

  • EHR A lifelong (longitudinal) electronic record across settings. More secure than paper: only authorized users, and access can be audited.
  • Eight IOM functions Data capture (history, labs, allergies, meds) · results management · order entry (CPOE) · decision support · electronic communication · patient support · administrative processes · reporting and population health.
  • CPOE Provider types the order: no illegible handwriting, flags duplicates and conflicts, auto-adds related orders.
  • Decision support Software that processes data to recommend choices: an alert that potassium (K) is 2.5 before digoxin, or suggesting fat emboli from entered findings.
  • Meaningful use Defined electronic health record (EHR) capabilities and standards hospitals must meet to get Medicare incentives.
  • Other tech Interoperability (systems share data) · point-of-care technology (looking up port-access steps on a handheld at the bedside) · telehealth (watching a patient's peritoneal dialysis (PD) exchange from another state) · biometrics (fingerprint log-in).
  • Barriers to one universal EHR Competing vendors and electronic health record (EHR) capability rolled out in stages.

Privacy, confidentiality, security

TermMeansExample
PrivacyThe patient's right to control their informationChoosing what to tell the provider
ConfidentialityKeeping shared information private unless others need to knowPledge to hold patient matters "in strict intimacy"
SecurityLimiting access with passwords, consent, and auditsMonthly audit of who opened records

HIPAA Protects confidentiality whatever the record type; breaches carry civil and criminal penalties.

Judging health information online

  • Domains .edu education · .gov government · .org nonprofit · .com commercial · .net internet provider · .mil military.
  • Criteria Authority (author credentials and contact info) · objectivity (who funds it; drug-company sponsor = bias) · accuracy · currency · coverage.
  • Searching Scholarly work → a research database like MEDLINE, not Google. Typing a guessed web address = a "brute force" search.
