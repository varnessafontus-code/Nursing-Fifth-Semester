Priority / red flag

Never pull out a penetrating object. Stabilize it in place.

Simple version

Your lungs are balloons inside a sealed box. If air or blood leaks into the box, the balloon can't blow up. A tension pneumothorax is the box filling so fast it squishes the heart too. That's why it's an emergency.

Thoracentesis

  • Before: signed consent, hold anticoagulants, baseline vital signs and oxygen saturation, chest x-ray done
  • Position: sitting on the edge of the bed, feet supported, leaning over the bedside table. If the client can't sit up, lie on the unaffected side with the head of bed at 45°
  • During: stay very still, no coughing or deep breaths. Monitor vital signs, oxygen saturation, and pain against baseline
  • After: chest x-ray, listen for absent or reduced breath sounds on the affected side, check the site for bleeding, watch for subcutaneous emphysema, infection, and tension pneumothorax

Simple version

The water seal is a one-way door. Air can bubble out, but it can't sneak back into the chest. Bubbling in the water seal that never stops means air is leaking somewhere. Find the leak.

NCLEX tip

With positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), or bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP), the priority assessment is bilateral breath sounds, because pressure can cause a pneumothorax.

Pulmonary embolism (PE)

  • Causes: a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) that breaks off; also air, fat, or amniotic fluid. Risks: immobility, surgery, dehydration, obesity, birth control pills, clotting disorders, atrial fibrillation (A-fib)
  • Signs: sudden shortness of breath, ↑ respiratory rate, low arterial oxygen (PaO₂), restlessness and apprehension, sharp chest pain, petechiae over the chest, hemoptysis, tachycardia
  • Diagnosis: ↑ D-dimer (shows a clot somewhere in the body), computed tomography angiogram (CTA) is the most common test (check renal function, dye), ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) scan (no dye), pulmonary angiography is the most specific
  • Prevention: turn every 2 hours, foot/knee/hip exercises, walk 4–6 times daily, compression stockings, fluids. No pneumatic compression devices if a deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is suspected
  • Treatment: oxygen per arterial blood gases (ABGs), anticoagulants (warfarin, heparin/enoxaparin, factor Xa inhibitors), fibrinolytics such as tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), embolectomy, inferior vena cava (IVC) filter. Warfarin: keep vitamin K (leafy greens) consistent, not eliminated

Common med-surg respiratory points (Iggy)

  • Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD): low-flow oxygen (1–2 L/min, target oxygen saturation 88–92%) for CO₂ retainers. A respiratory rate of 8 on 5 L/min means CO₂ narcosis, so act now. Tripod position, pursed-lip breathing, small frequent high-calorie meals, bronchodilator 30 minutes before meals
  • Asthma: rescue with short-acting beta-agonist (albuterol). A silent chest in a struggling patient is an emergency
  • Pneumonia: improving = respiratory rate normal, less dyspnea, white blood cell count (WBC) normalizing (5,000–10,000/mm³), fever down
  • Tuberculosis (TB): airborne precautions, N95 respirator, negative-pressure room. Patient wears a surgical mask during transport
  • Pulse oximetry: normal oxygen saturation 95–100%. Darker skin can read falsely high by 1–3 points. Poor perfusion, nail polish, and cold fingers cause errors

NCLEX tip

ABG ROME: Respiratory = pH and PaCO₂ move in Opposite directions; Metabolic = pH and HCO₃⁻ move the same direction (Equal). pH normal + both abnormal = fully compensated; pH abnormal + both abnormal = partially compensated. Example: pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 58, HCO₃⁻ 30 = partially compensated respiratory acidosis (classic chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD]). Arterial oxygen (PaO₂) below 60 = significant hypoxemia.

Priority / red flag

Never withhold oxygen from a hypoxic chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patient. Iggy: titrate to oxygen saturation (SpO₂) 88–92% and watch level of consciousness and ABGs. New confusion or drowsiness = CO₂ narcosis → escalate. A silent chest in asthma = no air moving. Suspected pulmonary embolism (PE): stay with the patient, head of bed up, oxygen, Rapid Response, prepare for anticoagulation.

  • Pneumonia: sputum and blood cultures before the first antibiotic dose, then give antibiotics without delay. Older adults: new confusion may be the only sign
  • Tuberculosis (TB): cough over 3 weeks, night sweats, afternoon low-grade fever, weight loss, hemoptysis. Sputum for acid-fast bacilli early morning × 3. Positive skin test (read at 48–72 hours; 10 mm or more positive for health care workers, 5 mm or more if immunocompromised) shows exposure, not active disease. Non-infectious after therapy, improvement, and 3 negative smears; directly observed therapy if adherence is a concern
  • Chest tube extras: never let drainage touch the tubing; tube pulled out → cover with sterile gauze immediately; crackling under the skin → mark the borders and notify; milk only if prescribed (never strip)
  • Tracheostomy suctioning: hyperoxygenate before and after, sterile technique, suction only on withdrawal, 10 seconds or less per pass, no more than 3 passes; obturator and spare trach at the bedside

Neurological assessment

Priority / red flag

Level of consciousness (LOC) is the most sensitive indicator of neuro status. A change in level of consciousness is often the first sign of trouble.

Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)Score
Eye opening: spontaneous / to voice / to pain / none4 / 3 / 2 / 1
Verbal: oriented / confused / inappropriate words / incomprehensible sounds / none5 / 4 / 3 / 2 / 1
Motor: obeys / localizes pain / withdraws / abnormal flexion (decorticate) / extension (decerebrate) / none6 / 5 / 4 / 3 / 2 / 1
  • Total 3–15. 13–15 is good. 8 or less → think intubation
  • Pupils: normal 2–6 mm, PERRLA (pupils equal, round, reactive to light and accommodation). Fixed and dilated = profound coma
  • Babinski sign (toes fan) is normal until age 1. In an adult it means an upper motor neuron problem (tumor, lesion, meningitis, multiple sclerosis [MS], amyotrophic lateral sclerosis [ALS])
  • Deep tendon reflexes: 0 absent, 1+ diminished, 2+ normal, 3+ brisk, 4+ hyperactive with clonus
Simple version

Level of consciousness is the brain's smoke alarm. Before anything else changes, the person gets sleepy, confused, or slow to answer. Notice it early.

Increased intracranial pressure (ICP)

Early signsLate signs
Change in level of consciousness (LOC) (earliest), shorter attention span, slurred or slow speech, slow response to commands, drowsiness, restlessness, confusion, headacheStupor → coma. Cushing's triad: rising systolic blood pressure (BP) with widening pulse pressure, slow bounding pulse, irregular respirations (Cheyne-Stokes or ataxic). Posturing: decorticate (arms flexed in) → decerebrate (all rigid extension = worse). Fixed dilated pupils, projectile vomiting
  • Treatment goals: reduce brain swelling, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and blood volume, while keeping the brain perfused
  • Keep oxygenated (low O₂ or high CO₂ dilates brain vessels → ↑ intracranial pressure). Avoid hypotension and bradycardia
  • Temperature below 100.4°F (38°C). Fever raises brain metabolism
  • Head of bed elevated 30°, head midline (lets the jugular veins drain). Avoid hip flexion, Valsalva, straining, coughing, nose blowing, and suctioning when possible. Space out nursing care
  • Medications: mannitol (osmotic diuretic), hypertonic saline 3%, dexamethasone (for tumors), barbiturate coma or propofol to lower metabolism, fluid restriction
  • Monitoring devices (ventricular catheter, subarachnoid bolt): greatest risk is infection. Keep dressings dry, no loose connections
  • Complications: brain herniation; diabetes insipidus (DI) or syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH), so assess for both
  • Any head injury client who starts complaining of a headache → assume intracranial pressure is rising
Simple version

The skull is a closed lunchbox. If the brain swells, there's nowhere to go, so it squishes itself. Everything we do is about letting pressure out and keeping blood and oxygen flowing in.

Lumbar puncture

  • Position: side-lying fetal position (chin to chest, knees up) or sitting bent over the table, to open the spaces between the vertebrae
  • Contraindicated with known increased intracranial pressure (ICP) (risk of brain herniation)
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) should be clear and colorless
  • After: lie flat 4–8 hours, increase fluids. Most common complication = headache that is worse sitting up and better lying flat → bed rest, fluids, analgesics, blood patch

Meningitis

  • Inflammation of the coverings of the brain and spinal cord (bacterial or viral)
  • Signs: high fever, chills, severe headache, nuchal rigidity, photophobia, nausea and vomiting, seizures, disorientation progressing to coma, positive Kernig sign (can't straighten the leg with the hip flexed) and positive Brudzinski sign (neck flexion makes hips and knees flex). Petechial rash suggests meningococcal disease
  • Bacterial: droplet precautions until 24 hours of antibiotics; contacts need prophylaxis. Viral: contact precautions (fecal-oral). Vaccine recommended for college students
  • Treatment: antibiotics (bacterial), corticosteroids, analgesics, anticonvulsants. A febrile newborn with petechiae is triaged first

Spinal cord injury: autonomic dysreflexia

Priority / red flag

Injury above T6. Sudden severe hypertension, pounding headache, bradycardia, flushing and sweating above the injury, nasal stuffiness, blurred vision. Risk of stroke. 1) Sit the client up. 2) Find and remove the trigger: distended bladder (most common, so check the catheter), constipation or impaction (topical anesthetic first), skin pressure, tight clothing, cold drafts. 3) Antihypertensive (hydralazine) if blood pressure (BP) stays high.

Simple version

The body below the injury is shouting "something hurts!" but the message can't reach the brain, so blood pressure shoots up. Sit them up and find what's bothering them, usually a full bladder.

Med-surg neuro highlights (Iggy)

  • Stroke (cerebrovascular accident [CVA]): time is brain. Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) generally within 3–4.5 hours of last known well, after computed tomography (CT) rules out bleeding. Aphasia: short simple phrases, normal volume. Dysphagia: swallow screen before any food or meds; keep nothing by mouth (NPO) if it fails
  • Seizures: protect from injury, turn to the side, time it, never put anything in the mouth. Status epilepticus = emergency (benzodiazepine)
  • Myasthenia gravis (MG): weakness worse with activity. Watch for respiratory failure (myasthenic vs cholinergic crisis)
  • Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS): cognition intact, death in 2–5 years usually from respiratory failure; riluzole slows it
  • Parkinson disease: fall risk, shuffling gait, so give non-skid tennis-type shoes, not slippers
  • Alzheimer disease: short-term memory loss is the earliest sign. Sumatriptan (migraine) is contraindicated with heart disease and uncontrolled hypertension

Priority / red flag

During alteplase: neuro checks and vital signs every 15 minutes. Stop the infusion and notify for a severe headache, severe hypertension, bleeding, or nausea and vomiting (suspect intracranial bleed). No nasogastric tubes or indwelling catheters for 24 hours. Avoid sudden drops in systolic BP.

  • Stroke sides: left hemisphere (right-sided weakness) = aphasia, slow and cautious, aware, depression. Right hemisphere (left-sided weakness) = impulsive, poor judgment, left neglect, highest fall risk. Hemianopsia: place items on the unaffected side, teach head scanning. Support the flaccid arm on a pillow
  • ICP care: avoid clustering care (space it out), hyperoxygenate before suctioning, stool softeners (no Valsalva), keep PaCO₂ normal
  • Seizures: status epilepticus (over 5 minutes) → airway, oxygen, IV lorazepam first, then fosphenytoin
  • TBI severity: mild GCS 13–15, moderate 9–12, severe 3–8
  • Spinal cord injury: C3–C5 run the diaphragm, so injuries at or above C4 often need ventilation. Neurogenic shock: hypotension + bradycardia + warm dry skin (T6 and above). Autonomic dysreflexia: sit up first, then bladder → bowel → skin
  • Myasthenia gravis (MG): pyridostigmine on time, 30–60 minutes before meals. Myasthenic crisis = too little drug; cholinergic crisis = too much (SLUDGE + bradycardia) → atropine. Avoid aminoglycosides and magnesium
  • Guillain-Barré: ascending paralysis after infection; monitor vital capacity; plasmapheresis or IV immune globulin
  • Parkinson disease: carbidopa-levodopa (orthostatic hypotension, avoid high-protein meals with doses). Multiple sclerosis (MS): avoid heat; baclofen (never stop abruptly). Alzheimer disease: donepezil (bradycardia)
  • Meningitis CSF: cloudy, high protein, low glucose, high white blood cells. Get cultures, then antibiotics as soon as possible

Orthopedics

Hurst manual pp. 174–182 · LaCharity Ch 11 · your Trends review guide

Fractures

  • Signs: pain and tenderness, abnormal movement, deformity, shortening of the extremity (muscle spasm), crepitus, swelling, bruising
  • First care: immobilize the joints above and below, move as little as possible, cover open fractures with something sterile
  • Most important assessment: neurovascular checks: pulses, color, movement, sensation, capillary refill, temperature (the 5 Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis)

Simple version

A cast is a tight sleeve. If the arm swells inside it, blood can't get in. Pain that medicine can't fix is the body saying "open the sleeve now."

Cast care

PlasterFiberglass
Takes 24–72 hours to dry. Handle with palms, not fingertips (dents cause pressure sores). Ice packs on the sides for the first 24 hours, keep uncovered to air dry, rest on a soft pillow (no plastic), circle and date any drainageDries in about 30 minutes, lightweight, stronger, better x-rays, earlier weight-bearing. Harder to mold
  • Itching: never stick anything inside the cast. Use a hair dryer on the cool setting
  • Pain → do neurovascular checks first. Pain not relieved by elevation, cold, and analgesics = think complication
  • Cast saw: it vibrates but does not cut skin

Traction

  • Purpose: reduce and immobilize fractures, relieve muscle spasm, prevent deformity
  • Weights hang freely, never resting on the bed or floor. Never remove traction without an order. Ropes move freely, knots tight. Keep the client pulled up and aligned
  • Skin traction (Buck's): tape or boot on the skin, short-term, used pre-op for hip fractures. Skin checks at least 3 times a day (heel and Achilles area)
  • Skeletal traction: pins, wires, tongs, halo, continuous. Pin site checks every 8 hours, pin care starting 48–72 hours after insertion. Small amount of clear (serous) drainage is expected; purulent drainage is not

Total hip replacement

  • Position: abduction (abductor pillow), neutral rotation (trochanter roll prevents external rotation), limit flexion to less than 90°. No crossing legs, no bending over, no low chairs, don't sleep on the operated side
  • Dislocation signs: shortened leg, abnormal rotation, can't move the leg, pain → keep still, call the provider
  • Infection prevention: prophylactic antibiotics, remove catheters and drains early. Also watch for deep vein thrombosis (DVT), avascular necrosis, immobility
  • Firm mattress, overhead trapeze, quad-setting exercises, no weight-bearing until ordered. Best exercise later: walking

Amputation

  • Tourniquet at the bedside (massive hemorrhage)
  • Prevent hip and knee flexion contractures: no pillows under the residual limb after 24 hours, lie prone several times a day, limb flat on the bed
  • Phantom pain is real. Try diversion first, then medication; it usually fades by about 3 months
  • Shape the limb into a cone for the prosthesis (elastic wrap), wear a limb sock, massage, toughen by pressing into soft, then firm surfaces. Strengthen upper body for crutches

Mobility aids

  • Walker: walk into it; rubber caps on all 4 legs
  • Crutches: 1–2 inches (2.5–5 cm) below the axilla, weight on the hands (not armpits, which risks brachial nerve damage). Stairs: up with the good, down with the bad
  • Cane: held on the strong side

Med-surg ortho highlights (Iggy)

  • Rheumatoid arthritis (RA): autoimmune, symmetric, morning stiffness more than 1 hour. Osteoarthritis (OA): wear and tear, asymmetric, worse with activity
  • Osteoporosis: calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, fall prevention. Bisphosphonates: take upright with water on an empty stomach, stay up 30 minutes
  • Scoliosis: sideways spine curve with uneven shoulders and hips

Added from your Trends review guide

  • Neurovascular checks: compare to the other limb. 6 Ps: pain, pressure, paresthesia, pallor, paralysis, pulselessness. Pulselessness is LATE; don't wait for it
  • Compartment syndrome: keep the limb at heart level (raising it above the heart lowers arterial inflow), loosen or bivalve the cast, notify immediately. Irreversible damage within 4–6 hours. After fasciotomy, dark tea-colored urine with low output = myoglobin → acute kidney injury
  • Fat embolism: 12–48 hours after a long-bone fracture. Earliest: hypoxemia, dyspnea, tachypnea and/or confusion; petechiae (neck, upper arms, chest) are classic but last. A blood-clot PE has no petechiae
  • Hip fracture: leg shortened and externally rotated, groin pain, can't bear weight. Older adult + fall = assume hip fracture
  • Hip replacement approach: anterior = lowest dislocation risk; posterolateral = highest. Precautions: no flexion over 90°, no crossing legs, no internal rotation; abduction pillow. Venous thromboembolism is the most life-threatening common complication → Pharmacology, Ambulation, Compression
  • Osteoporosis: DXA scan T-score −2.5 or lower; fall prevention is the priority
  • Gout: hot red big toe, high uric acid; acute: colchicine (diarrhea), nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), steroids; prevention: allopurinol; avoid purines (organ meats, shellfish, alcohol), fluids 2–3 L
  • Osteoarthritis (OA) vs rheumatoid arthritis (RA): OA asymmetric, weight-bearing joints, stiffness under 30 minutes, Heberden/Bouchard nodes. RA symmetric small joints, systemic, stiffness over 1 hour; methotrexate (weekly, folic acid, no alcohol, bone marrow and liver monitoring), biologics (TB screen first)
  • Osteomyelitis: bone pain, fever, high white blood cell count; weeks of IV antibiotics
  • Bisphosphonates (alendronate): first thing in the morning, full glass of water, empty stomach, upright 30 minutes, no food for 30 minutes. Raloxifene: DVT risk

Prenatal health, nutrition and teratogens

  • Folic acid 400–600 mcg/day (Hurst lists 400): leafy greens, fortified cereal, beans, citrus. Prevents spina bifida and anencephaly. Start before conception
  • Iron with vitamin C, not with milk, tea, or coffee. Expect black stools and constipation
  • High-dose vitamin A (retinol) is teratogenic. Food safety: no raw fish or meat, unpasteurized dairy, soft cheese, or cold deli meat (Listeria); limit high-mercury fish (shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish)
  • Weight gain (normal body mass index [BMI]) about 25–35 lb: 2–4 lb in the 1st trimester, then about 1 lb/week. Gaining 2 lb or more in a week → think preeclampsia
  • +300 calories/day in the 2nd trimester (+500 for adolescents). Protein 80–100 g/day. Exercise: walking and swimming, heart rate under 140, no hot tubs
  • Vaccines: inactivated flu and tetanus-diphtheria-pertussis (Tdap) are given in pregnancy. Live vaccines (measles-mumps-rubella [MMR], varicella) wait until postpartum
  • Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) testing is offered to every pregnant woman. Smoking: low birth weight, cleft lip or palate, and double the risk of placental abruption
  • Visits: monthly to 28 weeks, every 2 weeks to 36 weeks, then weekly. Gestational diabetes screen at 24–28 weeks. A temperature of 100.2 °F at a prenatal visit is not normal; notify the provider
  • Danger signs: gush of fluid, bleeding, abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, severe headache, visual changes, fever, sudden swelling, decreased fetal movement
Simple version

Early pregnancy is a construction site. Prenatal care is the building inspector: good materials in (folic acid, iron, food), wreckers out (alcohol, smoking, live vaccines, risky meds).

Body changes of pregnancy

  • Cardiovascular: blood volume up 40–50%, heart rate up 10–15 bpm. Plasma rises faster than red cells, causing physiologic (dilutional) anemia; true iron deficiency still gets treated. Clotting factors rise, so deep vein thrombosis (DVT) risk rises
  • Supine hypotension: the uterus compresses the vena cava → dizzy, pale, low BP, fetal distress. Fix first: left side or a wedge under the right hip
  • Respiratory: shortness of breath late in pregnancy is normal. Gastrointestinal: progesterone causes heartburn and constipation (small frequent meals, stay upright). Urinary frequency in the 1st and 3rd trimesters; higher urinary tract infection (UTI) risk
  • Skin: linea nigra, chloasma, striae, darker areolas are normal. Second trimester: quickening; nausea and frequency ease. Kegel exercises

Labor medications and procedures

  • Oxytocin (Pitocin), high-alert: 1-to-1 nurse, piggybacked at the port closest to the IV site, own labeled bag. Goal: contractions every 2–3 minutes lasting about 60 seconds. Stop for tachysystole, contractions too long, or fetal distress / late decelerations (keep the main IV running). Risk of uterine rupture (vaginal birth after cesarean [VBAC]) and water intoxication. Never flat on the back
  • Epidural: before: baseline vital signs, IV access, preload 500–1000 mL LR. Test dose: watch for metallic taste, mouth numbness, tinnitus, sudden dense block. After: BP every 5 minutes × 15 minutes, registered nurse (RN) stays 30 minutes; check FHR, block level, bladder (she can't feel it)
  • Epidural hypotension: reposition (left uterine displacement), fluid bolus, oxygen, notify anesthesia, ephedrine. Spinal headache: worse upright → bed rest, fluids, blood patch. Contraindications: clotting problems, uncorrected hypovolemia, infection at the site or severe systemic infection
  • Preterm labor (20–37 weeks): hydrate, treat infection, tocolytics. Nifedipine (headache, flushing, hypotension) and terbutaline (hold if maternal HR over 120; hyperglycemia, hypokalemia). Magnesium short-term for fetal neuroprotection. Betamethasone 24–34 weeks, 2 IM doses 24 hours apart, to mature fetal lungs

Hypertensive disorders

  • Preeclampsia: new BP 140/90 or higher after 20 weeks + proteinuria or end-organ signs. Watch headache, visual changes, epigastric or right upper quadrant (RUQ) pain, hyperreflexia and clonus, edema, rapid weight gain
  • Severe features: BP 160/110 or higher, persistent headache, scotomata, RUQ pain, pulmonary edema, urine output under 30 mL/hr, platelets under 100,000, high liver enzymes
  • HELLP: Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets. RUQ pain, nausea, "flu-like." Don't palpate the liver
  • Eclampsia = seizure: side-lying, protect the airway, don't restrain, oxygen, magnesium. Preeclampsia can also appear postpartum (headache not relieved by meds, flashing lights → come in)
  • Nursing: seizure precautions, quiet dim room, left side-lying, strict intake and output (Foley), daily weight, DTRs and clonus, lung sounds, fetal monitoring. Treatment: magnesium (prevents seizures; not an antihypertensive), labetalol or hydralazine for BP. Delivery is the cure
Priority / red flag

Magnesium toxicity, in order: DTRs lost → RR under 12 → cardiac arrest. Also urine output under 30 mL/hr and decreasing level of consciousness. Absent DTRs = stop the infusion and notify, even if RR is still OK. Antidote: calcium gluconate 10% IV, kept at the bedside.

Simple version

Magnesium is a dimmer switch for the nerves. It turns the brain down so it can't seize. Too far, and the reflexes go out first, then breathing, then the heart. Calcium gluconate turns the lights back up.

Diabetes, Rh, and infections

  • Gestational diabetes (GDM): screen at 24–28 weeks; insulin is the standard drug. Insulin needs drop fast after the placenta delivers. Lifelong higher type 2 diabetes risk, so teach follow-up screening
  • Infant of a diabetic mother (IDM): macrosomia → shoulder dystocia, birth injury; hypoglycemia (lowest at 1–3 hours); respiratory distress syndrome (insulin delays surfactant); polycythemia → jaundice; hypocalcemia
  • Rh: Rh-negative mother + Rh-positive baby. The first baby is usually fine; antibodies attack later pregnancies (erythroblastosis fetalis). RhoGAM at 28 weeks, within 72 hours after birth, and after any bleeding, trauma, amniocentesis or CVS, miscarriage, or ectopic. Only works before antibodies form, so not given if indirect Coombs is positive. Indirect Coombs = mother's blood; direct Coombs = baby's cord blood
  • Group B streptococcus (GBS): swab at 35–37 weeks; IV penicillin or ampicillin in labor every 4 hours, at least 4 hours before birth. Treated in labor, not during pregnancy
  • HIV in labor: avoid anything that breaks fetal skin (scalp electrode, forceps, vacuum, episiotomy). Epidural is fine. Bathe the baby before injections. No breastfeeding (U.S. guidance)
Simple version

RhoGAM erases the baby's blood cells from mom's memory before her immune system writes them down as enemies. Once written down, it can't be erased.

Key terms

  • Advance care plan: reflecting on what gives life value → goals of care. Two parts: the advance directive and the named decision-maker
  • Decision-making capacity: understands the facts, appreciates the alternatives, communicates a choice
  • Patient Self-Determination Act (PSDA, 1991): facilities must give written information on decision-making rights, ask about advance directives, and help complete them
  • Double effect: the intent is to relieve suffering (for example, escalating morphine), even though death is a foreseeable possible effect
  • Palliative sedation: for refractory symptoms. Educate and support the family
  • Medically futile care: treatment with no possible physiologic benefit
  • Prognostication: Palliative Performance Scale (PPS). Compare function at 12 months, 6 months, 1 month, and now
  • End-of-Life Nursing Education Consortium (ELNEC): 8 modules: nursing care at end of life, pain, symptoms, ethical/legal, cultural, communication, loss/grief/bereavement, final hours
  • National Consensus Project (NCP): 8 domains of quality palliative care
  • Legal right to refuse treatment: Quinlan and Cruzan cases (Cruzan → clear and convincing evidence → led to the Patient Self-Determination Act [PSDA])

Consult triggers

  • "Surprise question": would you be surprised if this patient died in 6–12 months? (No = consult)
  • Metastatic cancer, long-term care resident, cognitive impairment, no advance care planning, prior hospice, multifaceted care (ventilator, parenteral feeding)
  • Prolonged intensive care unit (ICU) stay, uncontrolled symptoms, family dissension, decisions about feeding tubes, tracheostomy, dialysis, or devices

Barriers and challenges

  • 4 barriers: (1) lack of understanding ("it's not time yet"), (2) too few trained professionals, (3) payment and reimbursement, (4) poor communication
  • Families may want intravenous (IV) fluids or antibiotics "for the family's sake". Decreased eating is natural at the end of life, and artificial nutrition can be a burden
  • End-of-life dyspnea → as-needed (PRN) morphine (relieves air hunger and anxiety). No rescue measures for a comfort-focused patient
  • Long-term opioids: constipation never develops tolerance
Simple version

Palliative care is a helper team that joins you early to make you feel better while you fight the illness. Hospice is the same kind of team for the last part of life, when the goal is only comfort.

Simple version

Being a nurse isn't just one job in a hospital. It's a toolbox you can take to schools, airplanes, churches, courtrooms, and people's homes.

Job search (Trends Ch 27)

Cherry Ch 27

  • Know yourself first: values, strengths, preferred setting. Look at Magnet hospitals (better work environments, more certified and baccalaureate-prepared nurses)
  • Ask employers for: registered nurse (RN) vacancy and turnover rate, patient and employee satisfaction, staff education mix, use of travelers, the latest Joint Commission report, union status
  • Quitting early earns a "job hopper" label and poor references, and turnover is expensive for hospitals

  • Interview: dress appropriately, arrive on time, bring your résumé. Phone and video interviews count too, and employers check social media
  • Behavioral question ("Tell me about a time when…") → specific story: situation, action, result
  • Good questions to ask: job description, nurse-to-patient ratio, support staff, how performance is evaluated, schedule, salary and differential, staff input in decisions
  • Applicants' rights: know the qualifications, fair consideration, schedule, benefits, see the unit and meet the manager, know the orientation, know when a decision is expected
  • Follow up: thank-you letter. Avoid impulse decisions. Weigh standards of practice, an acuity-adjusted ratio, structured new-graduate orientation, advancement, salary, benefits, continuing education
  • Employer checks: references, licensure, background, sometimes a clinical skills test. Be honest on applications
Simple version

A job interview goes both ways. They're checking if you fit, and you're checking if they'll take good care of you while you learn.
